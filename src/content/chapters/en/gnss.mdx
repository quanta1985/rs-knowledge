## 7.1 Measuring pseudoranges

<Term id="gnss">GNSS</Term> does not measure position directly. Each satellite carries atomic clocks and continuously broadcasts "who I am, where I am, what time it is". The receiver compares the transmit time with the receive time and multiplies by the speed of light to get a range. Because the receiver clock (a cheap quartz crystal) is not synchronised with the satellite clocks, that range carries a common error, hence the name <Term id="pseudorange">pseudorange</Term>.

The pseudorange equation to satellite i puts everything the receiver measures into one line:

<Formula label="Pseudorange equation" tex="\rho_i = \sqrt{(x_i - x)^2 + (y_i - y)^2 + (z_i - z)^2} + c\,\delta t + I_i + T_i + \varepsilon_i" where="
\rho_i :: measured pseudorange to satellite i (m)
x,\ y,\ z :: receiver position to solve for
x_i,\ y_i,\ z_i :: satellite position at transmission, from the broadcast ephemeris
c\,\delta t :: receiver clock error in metres, common to all satellites
I_i,\ T_i :: ionospheric and tropospheric delay on the path
\varepsilon_i :: multipath, receiver noise and residual satellite clock and orbit error
" />

<Note kind="key">

There are **four unknowns**: x, y, z and δt. So at least **four satellites** are needed. <mark class="key">The fourth satellite is not there for "more accuracy"; it is there to solve the receiver's own clock error.</mark>

</Note>

Figure 7.2 shrinks the problem to a plane: two coordinates plus a clock error is three unknowns, so three satellites are needed. When δt ≠ 0, every circle grows by the same c·δt and they no longer meet at one point.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: how long does a GPS signal travel?

1. A GPS satellite flies at about 20,200 km. At the zenith: t = 20,200 / 299,792 ≈ **67.4 ms**.
2. Near the horizon the range is √((6378 + 20,200)² − 6378²) ≈ 25,800 km, i.e. **86.1 ms**.
3. The receiver must measure these 70–90 ms to a few nanoseconds, one part in ten million.

</div>

<Note kind="myth">

**"My phone sends a signal up to the satellite, which works out my position and sends it back."** No. GNSS is **one-way**, from satellite to user. The receiver only listens; the position is computed in the device. The satellites do not know who is using them, so the number of users is unlimited and GNSS by itself cannot "track" anyone. If your location is shared, an app sent it over the mobile network, not GNSS.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Linearisation and the least-squares solution">

The range equation is nonlinear. Starting from an approximate position x₀ (even the Earth's centre), a first-order Taylor expansion gives:

<Formula label="Linearised model" tex="\Delta\boldsymbol{\rho} = H\,\Delta\mathbf{x}, \qquad H_i = \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf{e}_i^{\mathsf T} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf{x} = (H^{\mathsf T}H)^{-1}H^{\mathsf T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\rho}" where="
\Delta\boldsymbol{\rho} :: measured pseudoranges minus ranges computed from x₀
\mathbf{e}_i :: unit vector from the receiver to satellite i
\Delta\mathbf{x} :: correction to (x, y, z, c·δt)
" />

Add Δx to x₀ and repeat (Gauss–Newton). Because the satellites are far away, 3–5 iterations converge, as Figure 7.2 shows. The matrix (HᵀH)⁻¹ is also where DOP comes from in section 7.4.

</Depth>

The counts are every satellite in the CelesTrak group, **not filtered by health**: some are in maintenance, testing or retired, so fewer are actually usable. ### Regional systems: QZSS and NavIC

Two regional systems complement the four global ones in Asia:

- **QZSS** (Japan) uses inclined geosynchronous satellites tracing a figure of eight over East Asia and Australia, plus a geostationary one. Its signals are GPS-compatible, so to a receiver it is like "a few more GPS satellites high in the sky". QZSS also broadcasts two correction services: **CLAS** (centimetre-level, Japan only) and **MADOCA-PPP** (Asia-Oceania, including Viet Nam); see section 7.5.
- **NavIC** (India) uses geostationary and inclined geosynchronous satellites covering India and its surroundings; Viet Nam is outside its primary service area.

Since receivers started tracking several systems at once (<Term id="gnss-multi">multi-GNSS</Term>), the number of usable satellites has gone from about 8 to several dozen. Figure 7.3 shows where that helps most.

## 7.3 The four main error sources

A pseudorange is the true range plus a series of errors. The four largest groups are satellite clock and orbit, the ionosphere, the troposphere and multipath. Each has its own remedy.

### Satellite clocks and orbits

GPS satellites carry rubidium or caesium atomic clocks; Galileo adds passive hydrogen masers. Even so, the satellite positions and clocks a receiver uses are **predictions** computed by the control segment and uploaded, so they drift slightly from reality. The 2020 GPS performance standard commits to a signal-in-space range error (SIS URE) of at most **2.0 m** (95 %, global average, all ages of data); in practice it is usually much smaller. IGS post-processed products are accurate to a few centimetres, and they are the basis of PPP (section 7.5).

### Ionospheric delay

The ionosphere (about 60–1000 km) holds free electrons knocked loose by solar radiation. The code signal is slowed in proportion to the <Term id="tec">total electron content</Term> along the path and inversely to the square of the frequency:

<Formula label="First-order ionospheric delay (code)" tex="I = \frac{40.3\ \mathrm{TEC}}{f^2}\ \ [\mathrm{m}], \qquad 1\ \mathrm{TECU} = 10^{16}\ \mathrm{e/m^2} \;\Rightarrow\; I_{L1} = 0.162\ \mathrm{m/TECU}" where="
\mathrm{TEC} :: electrons in a 1 m² column along the path (electrons/m²)
f :: carrier frequency (Hz); L1 = 1575.42 MHz
40.3 :: constant from the plasma refractive index (m³/s²)
" />

By day in the tropics, in years of high solar activity, vertical TEC can reach 50–100 TECU, i.e. **8.1–16.2 m** on L1. A satellite at 30° elevation looks through the ionosphere at a slant, so the TEC on its path is 1.75 times larger: 50 TECU vertical becomes 14.2 m. Viet Nam lies under the northern crest of the <Term id="gnss-eia">equatorial ionisation anomaly</Term>, one of the highest-TEC regions in the world, and post-sunset scintillation can make receivers lose lock. In October 2024 NASA and NOAA announced that the Sun had reached the maximum phase of solar cycle 25; scintillation can stay strong for a few years after the peak.

Single-frequency receivers use a model broadcast in the navigation message: GPS uses the Klobuchar model, estimated to remove only **about 50 %** of the ionospheric error (RMS); Galileo uses NeQuick-G. The thorough fix is to measure on two frequencies:

<Predict client:visible lang="en"
  title="How much more does the ionosphere delay L5 than L1?"
  q="L1 = 1575.42 MHz, L5 = 1176.45 MHz. For the same TEC, the ionospheric delay on L5 compared with L1 is:"
  options={['The same', 'About 1.3×', 'About 1.8×', 'About 3×']} answer={2}
  why="Delay scales as 1/f², so the ratio is (1575.42 / 1176.45)² = 1.793. Because the delay differs by frequency, measuring on two frequencies lets you solve for it and remove it." />

<Formula label="Ionosphere-free combination" tex="P_{IF} = \frac{f_1^2 P_1 - f_2^2 P_2}{f_1^2 - f_2^2} \approx 2.26\,P_{L1} - 1.26\,P_{L5}" where="
P_1,\ P_2 :: pseudoranges on the two frequencies f_1, f_2 (m)
P_{IF} :: pseudorange with the first-order ionospheric delay removed
" />

The first-order delay (over 99 % of the total) cancels completely. The price is noise: for L1/L5 the coefficients are 2.26 and −1.26, so the noise of P_IF is about **2.59 times** the single-frequency noise (for equal, independent noise). Dual frequency therefore pays off most when measurement noise is low: the fast-chipping L5 signal, a good antenna, little multipath.

### Tropospheric delay

The lower atmosphere (0 to about 12 km) slows **all frequencies equally**, so dual frequency cannot remove it. The delay has a **dry** (hydrostatic, about 90 %) part that depends on pressure and a **wet** part from water vapour. The dry part at the zenith follows very well from surface pressure:

<Formula label="Tropospheric delay: Saastamoinen model and mapping function" tex="T(E) \approx \big(\mathrm{ZHD} + \mathrm{ZWD}\big)\,m(E), \qquad \mathrm{ZHD} = \frac{0.0022768\,P}{1 - 0.00266\cos 2\varphi - 0.00028\,H}, \qquad m(E) \approx \frac{1}{\sin E}" where="
P :: surface pressure (hPa)
\varphi,\ H :: station latitude and height (km)
\mathrm{ZHD},\ \mathrm{ZWD} :: zenith hydrostatic and wet delays (m)
m(E) :: mapping function of elevation E; 1/sin E is good to about 15°
" />

At Hanoi, with 1013.25 hPa, the zenith dry delay is **2.31 m**; at Sa Pa (1.6 km, about 840 hPa assumed) it is 1.92 m. At 30° elevation the path is twice as long, 4.62 m. At 5°, 1/sin E gives a factor of 11.5, about 26 m (standard mapping functions such as Niell give a little less). The wet part is only a few tens of centimetres but varies strongly, especially in Viet Nam's rainy season, and is the hardest residual error for RTK and PPP. Conversely, because it can be measured, the wet delay at CORS stations becomes a water-vapour data source for weather forecasting (section 7.7).

### Multipath

Signals reflected by glass walls, water and road surfaces reach the antenna together with the direct signal. <Term id="multipath">Multipath</Term> cannot be modelled remotely because it depends on the surroundings of each antenna. According to ESA Navipedia, code multipath is usually **under 2–3 m**, but can in theory reach 1.5 code chips. On the carrier phase it never exceeds a quarter wavelength (about 5 cm on L1) and is usually under 1 cm.

The worst case in high-rise streets is <Term id="gnss-nlos">NLOS</Term>: the direct ray is blocked and the receiver only gets a reflection. The pseudorange then grows by the whole extra path, possibly tens of metres, with no direct ray to "pull" it back. Figure 7.5 computes the extra path for a street cross-section.

Figure 7.5 shows three cases: direct ray only (good), direct plus reflection (multipath, an error that is a fraction of the extra path), and reflection only (NLOS, the whole extra path, e.g. **16.1 m** in the high-rise section). Short-chip signals such as L5 (29.3 m) help: reflections delayed by more than about 1.5 chips (44 m) hardly bias the correlator, whereas with L1 C/A (293 m) every reflection in a street falls inside the zone of influence. <mark>Multipath can only be reduced on site: a good antenna, an open location, short-chip signals and algorithms that drop suspected NLOS satellites.</mark>

<Note kind="tip">

Place survey antennas away from walls, metal roofs and water; use a choke-ring antenna for permanent stations; and set a 10–15° mask to drop low rays that both reflect easily and cross the most atmosphere.

</Note>

## 7.4 DOP: geometry decides accuracy

For the same range error, the computed position can be good or bad depending on where the satellites are in the sky. If they cluster on one side, the spheres intersect at shallow angles and a small error pushes the intersection far away. <Term id="dop">DOP</Term> measures that amplification, multiplying the <Term id="gnss-uere">UERE</Term>, the user equivalent range error:

<Formula label="Position error = geometry × range error" tex="\sigma_{H} = \mathrm{HDOP}\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}}, \qquad \sigma_{V} = \mathrm{VDOP}\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}}" where="
\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}} :: standard deviation of the pseudorange error (m), the same for all satellites
\sigma_H,\ \sigma_V :: horizontal and vertical position error (1σ)
" />

DOP comes from the design matrix H of section 7.1, written in the local East–North–Up frame (E, N, U) plus a clock column:

<Formula label="DOP from the matrix Q" tex="Q = (H^{\mathsf T}H)^{-1}, \quad \mathrm{GDOP} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr} Q}, \quad \mathrm{PDOP} = \sqrt{q_{EE} + q_{NN} + q_{UU}}, \quad \mathrm{HDOP} = \sqrt{q_{EE} + q_{NN}}, \quad \mathrm{VDOP} = \sqrt{q_{UU}}, \quad \mathrm{TDOP} = \sqrt{q_{TT}}" where="
H_i :: row i: (−cos E sin A, −cos E cos A, −sin E, 1) with azimuth A and elevation E
q_{EE},\ q_{NN},\ q_{UU},\ q_{TT} :: diagonal elements of Q for East, North, Up and clock
" />

PDOP is for 3D position, HDOP horizontal, VDOP vertical, TDOP the clock, and GDOP all of them. Almost everywhere VDOP is larger than HDOP, because no satellite below the horizon "pins" the height from underneath: at Hanoi, GPS with a 10° mask, the median 24-hour HDOP is 0.89 and VDOP 1.56. The rating below is a common convention, not a standard:

<div class="table-wrap">

| DOP | Rating | Good enough for |
|---|---|---|
| ≤ 1 | Ideal | Everything |
| 1–2 | Good | Surveying, precise navigation |
| 2–5 | Moderate | Consumer mapping, car navigation |
| 5–10 | Poor | Rough guidance only |
| > 10 | Unreliable | Discard |

</div>

#### Example: one phone, two streets

1. The phone's range error is about 3 m (UERE). The open sky of Figure 7.6 has an HDOP of **0.99**, so the horizontal 1σ error ≈ 0.99 × 3 = **3.0 m**.
2. In the north–south street canyon the two low satellites to the East and West are blocked and HDOP rises to **6.61**: horizontal 1σ ≈ 6.61 × 3 = **19.8 m**, before any multipath.
3. The error ellipse stretches East–West (major axis at azimuth 89°), exactly where satellites are missing. That is why GPS fixes in narrow streets jump to the parallel street rather than along the street.

</div>

### From error budget to position error

UERE combines the independent errors of section 7.3 as a root-sum-square:

<Formula label="Range error budget (UERE)" tex="\sigma_{\mathrm{UERE}} = \sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{sat}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{iono}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{tropo}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{mp+noise}}^2}" where="

\sigma_{\mathrm{tropo}} :: unmodelled wet part, 0.1 m at the zenith × 1/sin E
\sigma_{\mathrm{mp+noise}} :: depends on the environment; ×2.59 with the ionosphere-free combination
" />

For a single-frequency receiver in open sky at Hanoi (TEC 30 TECU, typical elevation 45°, GPS, 10° mask), the ionosphere leaves 3.28 m and dominates the UERE (3.48 m). The 95 % horizontal error is about **6.2 m** and vertical **10.6 m**, within the GPS SPS standard (≤ 8 m horizontal, ≤ 13 m vertical, global average). With dual frequency, UERE drops to 1.65 m and the 95 % horizontal error to **3.0 m**. But in a high-rise street (5 m multipath, 30° mask), the ionosphere-free combination multiplies multipath by 2.59 and comes out **worse** than single frequency (41.8 m against 19.5 m, horizontal 95 %).

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">Position error = DOP × UERE.</mark> To improve, reduce either one: more satellites and a lower mask for a smaller DOP, or remove range errors with dual frequency, models and corrections (section 7.5). Where multipath is heavy, removing the ionosphere does not help; deal with multipath first.

</Note>

## 7.5 RTK, PPP and SBAS

Standalone code positioning (like an ordinary phone) reaches a few metres. Doing better means removing the errors of section 7.3, in one of three ways: differencing with a nearby station (RTK), using precise global satellite orbits and clocks (PPP), or receiving regional corrections through geostationary satellites (SBAS).

<div class="table-wrap">

| Technique | Principle | Needs | Horizontal accuracy | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standalone (code) | Solve 4 unknowns from pseudoranges | One receiver | ≤ 8 m (95 %, GPS SPS standard) | Instant |
| <Term id="sbas">SBAS</Term> | Geostationary satellites broadcast regional corrections and integrity | SBAS-capable receiver inside the service area | ≤ 3 m (95 %, EGNOS Open Service) | Instant |
| <Term id="ppp">PPP</Term> | Precise global orbits and clocks, every error modelled | Dual-frequency receiver, correction products | 0.2–0.3 m (95 %, HAS, B2b, MADOCA) | Minutes to 30 min |
| <Term id="rtk">RTK</Term> | Difference with a nearby base, measure <Term id="carrier-phase">carrier phase</Term> | Base or CORS network within tens of km, data link | 2–4 cm (VNGEONET, tested) | Once integers are fixed |

### 7.5.1 RTK: differencing with a nearby station

RTK works because a nearby base and rover suffer almost the same satellite clock, ionospheric and tropospheric errors; differencing cancels them. On top of that RTK uses the <Term id="carrier-phase">carrier phase</Term> (19 cm wavelength on L1), not just the code, so once the integer number of cycles is known the accuracy reaches centimetres.

<Steps title="How RTK works" items={[
  { tag: '1', t: 'The base knows its exact coordinates', d: 'Its antenna sits on a surveyed mark and observes the same satellites as the rover.' },
  { tag: '2', t: 'Send observations or corrections', d: 'The base streams data in the RTCM format, usually over the Internet with the NTRIP protocol, or by radio.' },
  { tag: '3', t: 'Double differences', d: 'The rover differences between the two receivers and between two satellites: satellite and receiver clock errors cancel, the atmosphere almost cancels.' },
  { tag: '4', t: 'Fix the integers', d: 'Solve the integer cycle ambiguities of the carrier phase. Before fixing the solution is "float" (dm); after, "fixed" (cm).' },
  { tag: '5', t: 'Continuous positioning', d: 'Centimetre positions every second, as long as phase lock and the correction stream hold.' },
]} />

The farther from the base, the more the atmosphere differs between the two sites and the worse the accuracy. **Network RTK** solves this: many continuously operating reference stations (<Term id="gnss-cors">CORS</Term>) form a grid, a server interpolates the errors and sends the rover a "virtual reference station" (VRS) right next to it.

In Viet Nam the national satellite positioning network **VNGEONET** is operated by the Viet Nam Department of Surveying, Mapping and Geographic Information (Cục Đo đạc, Bản đồ và Thông tin địa lý Việt Nam). According to the Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment (December 2019), it has **65 CORS stations**: 24 Geodetic CORS 150–200 km apart that anchor the national coordinate frame, and 41 NRTK CORS 50–80 km apart in three regions (Red River Delta and Thanh Hóa, Central and Central Highlands, the South), with tested RTK accuracy of **2–4 cm**. At the time the Department planned about 90–100 more NRTK stations. Commercial CORS networks and provincial or company networks exist alongside.

  { v: '65', l: 'VNGEONET CORS stations (24 Geodetic + 41 NRTK), 2019' },
  { v: '2–4', u: 'cm', l: 'tested RTK accuracy on VNGEONET' },
  { v: '50–80', u: 'km', l: 'spacing of the NRTK stations' },

<Note kind="warn">

RTK corrections give coordinates in the **base station's frame**. Mixing VN-2000 and ITRF/WGS84 shifts points by tens of centimetres to a few metres (Chapter 10). Always state the frame and epoch when delivering data.

</Note>

### 7.5.2 PPP: one receiver, global corrections

<Term id="ppp">PPP</Term> needs no nearby base. It uses precise satellite orbits and clocks (from IGS or a service provider), the ionosphere-free combination of code and phase, and estimates everything else.

<Steps title="How PPP works" items={[
  { tag: '1', t: 'A global network computes products', d: 'Dozens to hundreds of stations worldwide compute precise orbits, satellite clocks and signal biases.' },
  { tag: '2', t: 'Broadcast the corrections', d: 'Over the Internet, or in the satellite signal itself: Galileo E6 (HAS), BeiDou B2b, QZSS L6 (MADOCA, CLAS).' },
  { tag: '3', t: 'Remove the ionosphere', d: 'A dual-frequency receiver forms ionosphere-free code and carrier-phase combinations.' },
  { tag: '4', t: 'Estimate everything together', d: 'Position, receiver clock, wet tropospheric delay and the phase ambiguities.' },
  { tag: '5', t: 'Converge', d: 'The solution improves as the satellite geometry changes. Fixing integers (PPP-AR) or adding regional atmospheric corrections (PPP-RTK) converges faster.' },
]} />

More and more PPP corrections are broadcast **free, straight from the satellites**:

- **Galileo HAS** (High Accuracy Service): the initial service started on 24 Jan 2023. The Service Level 1 targets are 20 cm horizontal and 40 cm vertical (95 %) with 300 s convergence; with Galileo + GPS the typical values are ≤ 15 cm horizontal and ≤ 20 cm vertical (68 %). But the Service Definition Document (SDD Issue 1.0) **excludes 90°–180°E, 60°S–60°N from its commitments**, which includes Viet Nam: users here can still use it, without a performance guarantee.
- **BeiDou PPP-B2b**: broadcast by three BeiDou-3 GEO satellites. The performance standard (v3.0, 2021) commits to ≤ 0.3 m horizontal and ≤ 0.6 m vertical (95 %), convergence ≤ 30 min, over 10°–55°N, 75°–135°E. Most of Viet Nam lies inside, except the far south (Cà Mau at about 8.6°N) and the Trường Sa archipelago.
- **QZSS MADOCA-PPP**: operational since 1 Apr 2024 over Asia-Oceania, with a target under 30 cm horizontal (95 %); Internet distribution since 1 Jul 2024. **CLAS** reaches centimetres but only for Japan.

<Stats items={[
  { v: '20', u: 'cm', l: 'Galileo HAS: 95 % horizontal target, 300 s convergence' },
  { v: '0.3', u: 'm', l: 'BeiDou PPP-B2b: 95 % horizontal, convergence ≤ 30 min' },
  { v: '30', u: 'cm', l: 'QZSS MADOCA-PPP: 95 % horizontal target' },
]} caption="Galileo HAS SDD 1.0 (2023); BDS OS Performance Standard v3.0 (2021); MADOCA target (ICG 2022). These are commitments or targets, not measurements in Viet Nam." />

PPP is the low-cost choice for precision farming, structure monitoring and vessels far from CORS stations, at the price of waiting for convergence.

<Note kind="key">

The three augmentations trade accuracy, time and infrastructure: **RTK** gives centimetres almost at once but needs a base within tens of kilometres; **PPP** gives a few decimetres anywhere but needs time to converge; **SBAS** gives a few metres instantly, with integrity, but only inside its service area.

</Note>

### 7.5.3 SBAS: regional corrections via geostationary satellites

<Term id="sbas">SBAS</Term> was built for aviation: besides corrections it broadcasts **integrity**, telling the aircraft when not to trust a satellite.

<Steps title="How SBAS works" items={[
  { tag: '1', t: 'Reference station network', d: 'Dozens of stations across a continent monitor every GNSS satellite.' },
  { tag: '2', t: 'Master station computes corrections', d: 'Orbit and satellite clock corrections and an ionospheric delay grid, with error bounds for each satellite.' },
  { tag: '3', t: 'Uplink to a geostationary satellite', d: 'The GEO rebroadcasts on L1 with a GPS-like signal.' },
  { tag: '4', t: 'The receiver applies them', d: 'No Internet needed; the receiver just needs SBAS enabled and to be inside the service area.' },
]} />

Current SBAS: WAAS (US), EGNOS (Europe), MSAS (Japan), GAGAN (India), BDSBAS (China), KASS (Korea), SouthPAN (Australia, New Zealand). The EGNOS Open Service commits to ≤ 3 m horizontal and ≤ 4 m vertical (95 %, at the worst user location). GEO signals from GAGAN or BDSBAS can be received in Viet Nam, but **no SBAS has an official service area covering Viet Nam**, so here SBAS is at best an unofficial augmentation.

<Note kind="why" label="Link to remote sensing">

Every Earth-observation satellite such as Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 carries a dual-frequency GNSS receiver for precise orbit determination: if you do not know where the satellite is, you do not know where the pixel is. On the ground, ground control points (GCPs) for image rectification and DEM checks (Chapter 17) are surveyed with RTK.

</Note>

## 7.6 GNSS in phones and sensor fusion

A phone has a small, linearly polarised antenna held next to a body, so signals are weak and multipath is heavy. Four changes have brought phones closer to professional receivers:

<Tiles
  items={[
    { t: 'Multi-GNSS', d: 'GPS + Galileo + GLONASS + BeiDou (+ QZSS): dozens of satellites instead of about 8' },
    { t: 'Dual frequency L1 + L5', d: 'Since 2018 (Xiaomi Mi 8, Broadcom BCM47755 chip): L5 chips are 10× shorter, resisting multipath better' },
    { t: 'A-GNSS', d: 'The mobile network supplies ephemeris and an approximate position: a fix in seconds' },
    { t: 'Raw measurements', d: 'Since Android 7 (API 24, 2016) apps can read raw measurements and compute pseudoranges themselves' },
  ]}
  caption="Four advances of phone GNSS. Sources: EUSPA (2017) on Android raw measurements; IS-GPS-705 on the L5 chip rate."
/>

- **Dual frequency.** L5 has a 10.23 MHz chip rate, 10 times the L1 C/A code, so a chip is only 29.3 m long instead of 293 m. The correlation peak is sharper and tells reflections apart better (Figure 7.5); combined with L1 it also removes the ionosphere in open sky.
- **A-GNSS.** Without help the receiver must decode the 50 bit/s message itself, which takes up to about 30 seconds. With A-GNSS the ephemeris comes over the mobile network and the time to first fix drops to a few seconds.
- **Raw measurements.** Apps can read the receive time, Doppler, C/N0 and, on some phones, carrier phase. This opens the way to PPP and RTK on phones, and to inspection apps such as GPSTest.

<Note kind="myth">

**"Phone GPS needs the Internet (3G/4G) to work."** No. A GNSS receiver only needs the satellite signals, so it still gets a fix at sea or in mountains without coverage. The Internet only helps **A-GNSS** (a faster first fix) and loads **maps**; for offline navigation, download offline maps beforehand.

</Note>

In tunnels, under flyovers or between tall buildings, a phone does not rely on GNSS alone:

<Steps title="Sensor fusion when the signal is weak" items={[
  { tag: 'GNSS', t: 'Absolute position when available', d: 'Position and velocity from satellites, with a confidence estimate.' },
  { tag: 'IMU', t: 'Bridge gaps with inertia', d: 'Accelerometers and gyroscopes carry distance and heading forward (dead reckoning) in a tunnel; the error grows with time.' },
  { tag: 'Barometer', t: 'Floor height', d: 'A barometer measures height changes of a few metres, compensating for the large VDOP.' },
  { tag: 'Wi-Fi', t: 'Indoor positioning', d: 'Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and cellular fingerprints replace GNSS indoors.' },
  { tag: 'Map', t: 'Map matching', d: 'Snap GNSS points to the road network, often with a hidden Markov model, so the car does not "drive" across rooftops.' },
]} />

Phone GNSS data is the main input to mobility analytics (ride-hailing, traffic, tourism). Urban GNSS points are often 3–15 m off, so they must be map-matched before analysis. The classic method uses a hidden Markov model, with road segments as hidden states and GNSS points as noisy observations (Newson and Krumm, 2009).

## 7.7 Applications and limits

Beyond positioning and navigation, GNSS is invisible infrastructure for many systems:

- **Time synchronisation**: 4G/5G base stations, power grids, trading venues and data centres take their time from GNSS. Many people do not know they depend on it.
- **Surveying and deformation monitoring**: CORS stations measure tectonic motion and subsidence of millimetres per year, providing checkpoints for InSAR (Chapter 16); fixed receivers monitor dams, bridges and landslide slopes. In the Mekong Delta and Hanoi, GNSS and InSAR together measure land subsidence.
- **GNSS meteorology**: the wet tropospheric delay at CORS stations converts to precipitable water vapour (PWV) for rain forecast models. Radio-occultation satellites such as COSMIC-2 profile atmospheric temperature and humidity from bent GNSS signals.
- **GNSS reflectometry (GNSS-R)**: NASA's 8-microsatellite CYGNSS constellation measures GPS signals reflected by the sea to estimate hurricane winds, and over land to estimate soil moisture and flooding.
- **Precision agriculture, autosteering**: tractors, transplanters and spraying drones use centimetre RTK.

<Stats items={[
  { v: '−158.5', u: 'dBW', l: 'minimum received GPS L1 C/A power at the ground, about 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ W' },
  { v: 'Feb 2022', l: 'when EASA saw GNSS jamming and spoofing start to rise sharply around conflict zones' },
]} caption="IS-GPS-200 (minimum received power); EASA Safety Information Bulletin 2022-02R3 (5 Jul 2024)." />

GNSS signals reach the ground extremely weak, below the background noise. That makes them easy to **jam**: a low-power transmitter can blank receivers over a wide radius; and to **spoof**: transmit fake signals so a receiver computes a wrong position or time. The European Union Aviation Safety Agency's bulletin (EASA SIB 2022-02R3, July 2024) reports jamming and spoofing increasing since February 2022, especially around the Black Sea, the Baltic Sea, the Eastern Mediterranean, the Middle East and the Arctic, and stresses that spoofing is riskier than jamming because it is harder to detect.

<Note kind="warn">

Countermeasures: multi-frequency, multi-system receivers; navigation message authentication (Galileo OSNMA); multi-element anti-jam antennas; and IMU fusion to detect implausible position jumps. Critical infrastructure (timing for power grids, telecoms) should always have a non-GNSS backup.

</Note>

Where GNSS does **not** do well:

- **Indoors, tunnels, underground car parks**: signals do not penetrate; Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or UWB are needed.
- **Dense cities**: multipath, NLOS and a high mask (sections 7.3, 7.4); multi-GNSS, L5 and an IMU help.
- **Under dense forest canopy**: signals are attenuated and errors grow.
- **Height**: worse than horizontal (VDOP > HDOP), and it is height above the WGS84 ellipsoid, not above sea level (Chapter 10 on coordinate systems and VN-2000).
- **Geomagnetic storms and ionospheric scintillation** in the equatorial zone after sunset: receivers lose lock and RTK/PPP lose their integer fix.

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">GNSS is accurate and free but fragile: the signal is so weak that a small device can block or fake it.</mark> Any system that needs a trustworthy position or time needs a backup.

</Note>
