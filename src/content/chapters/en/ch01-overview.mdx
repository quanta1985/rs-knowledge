## 1.1 What remote sensing is

<Term id="remote-sensing">Remote sensing</Term> means gathering information about an object **without touching it**. In this handbook the "object" is land, water, vegetation and cities; "without touching" means measuring electromagnetic energy from a satellite hundreds of kilometres away.

Following the classic presentation of the Canada Centre for Remote Sensing (CCRS), every remote-sensing system has seven stages, from energy source to application. There are two ways to get the energy:

- A <Term id="passive-sensor">passive sensor</Term> measures energy that is already there: reflected sunlight (Sentinel-2, Landsat) or thermal radiation emitted by the surface itself (Landsat's thermal bands).
- An <Term id="active-sensor">active sensor</Term> emits its own energy and records the echo. Sentinel-1 radar (Chapter 11) is an example: it images at night and through clouds.

Sunlight crosses the atmosphere **twice** (down and up), so optical images always carry atmospheric effects (Chapters 3 and 7). Thermal energy crosses it once, from the surface up. Radar also crosses it twice, but the atmosphere is almost transparent to microwaves.

## 1.2 The four segments of a satellite system

A satellite does not make maps on its own. It is one link in a four-segment system:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Segment | What it contains | Sentinel-2 example |
|---|---|---|
| Launch | Rockets, launch sites | Sentinel-2A launched on a Vega rocket from Kourou (French Guiana) in 2015 |
| Space | **Bus** (structure, power, attitude control, thermal, communications) and **payload** (the instrument) | The payload is the MultiSpectral Instrument (MSI), 13 bands |
| Ground | Control centre, receiving antennas, processing and archiving | ESA's <Term id="ground-segment">ground segment</Term> produces L1C and L2A |
| User | People and software that use the data | You, opening an image in Earth Engine or QGIS |

</div>

Users usually see only the last segment, but data quality depends on all four: the orbit is kept by the launcher and thrusters, the sensor is calibrated in orbit, and the ground segment decides which <Term id="data-level">processing level</Term> you download.

<Depth level="ref" title="Reference: processing levels">

<div class="table-wrap">

| Level | Content | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| L0 | Raw data from the satellite, uncalibrated | Rarely distributed |
| L1 | Radiometrically calibrated and geometrically corrected; values are **top-of-atmosphere** (TOA) radiance or reflectance | Sentinel-2 L1C, Landsat Collection 2 Level-1, Sentinel-1 GRD |
| L2 | Atmospherically corrected; **surface** reflectance or surface temperature | Sentinel-2 L2A, Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 |
| L3, L4 | Temporal composites or modelled variables | Cloud-free mosaics, land-cover maps |

</div>

Chapters 4, 6 and 11 use L2A, Collection 2 Level-2 and GRD. Chapter 7 compares L1C with L2A.

</Depth>

All of them fly at 680–790 km with an inclination near 98°. This is a <Term id="sun-synchronous-orbit">sun-synchronous orbit</Term>: the satellite passes each place at the same local time, so illumination is similar from image to image. Chapter 2 explains what is special about 98°.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: Sentinel-2A's altitude from its orbital elements

CelesTrak gives Sentinel-2A's mean motion: n = 14.3081738 revolutions per day.

1. Period: T = 1440 min / 14.3081738 = **100.64 min**.
2. Kepler's third law: a = ∛(μ T² / 4π²), with μ = 398,600.4 km³/s² and T = 6038.5 s, gives a = **7167.1 km**.
3. Altitude above the 6378.1 km equatorial radius: h = 7167.1 − 6378.1 = **789.0 km**.
4. Velocity: v = √(μ / a) = **7.46 km/s**, about 26,900 km/h.

ESA quotes a mean altitude of 786 km for Sentinel-2. The 3 km difference comes from the reference radius (equatorial versus local).

</div>

## 1.4 How large is one scene?

Each acquisition records a strip as wide as the sensor's <Term id="swath">swath</Term>. Providers cut it into **scenes** or **tiles** for distribution. Scene size decides how many images you need to cover a study area.

One Landsat scene stretches about 190 km, from the northern mountains to beyond Hanoi. A VNREDSat-1 image covers only part of the inner city, but with 4 times finer pixels (2.5 m versus 10 m). Chapter 4 discusses the swath–resolution trade-off.

## 1.5 Milestones, and remote sensing in Viet Nam

<div class="table-wrap">

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1957 | Sputnik 1 (USSR), the first artificial satellite |
| 1972 | Landsat 1, start of the longest continuous land-imaging record |
| 1978 | Seasat, the first civilian satellite carrying a SAR |
| 2008 | USGS opens the whole Landsat archive free of charge |
| 2013 | **VNREDSat-1** launched on 7 May, Viet Nam's first Earth-observation satellite |
| 2014–2015 | Sentinel-1A (radar) and Sentinel-2A (multispectral), free and open data |

</div>

Source: Sổ tay Vệ tinh & Viễn thám (CC BY 4.0).

VNREDSat-1 is operated by the Vietnam National Space Center (VNSC) and acquires 2.5 m panchromatic and 10 m multispectral images. The November 2026 elements show something interesting: its actual inclination of 97.89° is **below** the sun-synchronous value for its altitude (98.10°). By the J2 formula (Chapter 2) its orbit plane turns 0.961°/day instead of 0.986°/day. The plane lags the Sun, so the overpass time gets **earlier**, by about 36 minutes per year if uncorrected. This is an estimate from one element set; the handbook has no VNSC operations data to confirm it.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Terra is drifting too">

Terra (carrying MODIS) has an inclination of 97.93°, also below the sun-synchronous value (98.14°). NASA stopped maintaining Terra's orbit as the mission entered its final phase, so its overpass time is getting earlier. When you compare long MODIS Terra records, remember that the illumination is no longer as fixed as in the early years.

</Depth>
