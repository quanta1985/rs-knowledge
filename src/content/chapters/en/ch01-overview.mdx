## 1.1 What a satellite is, and why it does not fall

An **artificial satellite** is an object people have put into motion around the Earth and that is kept there by gravity itself. It sounds odd, but a satellite is **always falling** towards the Earth. It never hits the ground because it moves sideways fast enough: while it falls a little, the curved surface below it drops away by exactly as much.

<Predict client:visible lang="en"
  title="How much gravity is left on the ISS?"
  q="The International Space Station flies at about 420 km. What percentage of surface gravity is left there?"
  slider={{ min: 0, max: 100, step: 1, unit: '%' }} answer={88} tol={4}
  why="Gravity falls with the square of the distance to the Earth's centre: (6371 / 6791)² ≈ 0.88, about 88 %. Astronauts float not because gravity is gone, but because they and the station fall freely with the same acceleration." />

The idea goes back to the thought experiment of "Newton's cannon": fire a cannonball from a mountain top faster and faster and it lands farther and farther away, until at some speed it falls around the Earth for ever. Chapter 3 solves the cannon problem numerically. Here we only look at **one second** of that motion.

<Figure
  title="Figure 1.2 — One second in orbit: how far it falls, how far the sphere curves away"

  source="Spherical Earth of radius 6371 km, μ = 398,600.4 km³/s², altitude 400 km; fall = ½·g·t² with g = μ/r², curvature drop = r − √(r² − (v·t)²) (src/lib/overview.ts, fallDrops)."
>
  <FallAround client:visible lang="en" />
</Figure>

At 400 km gravity pulls the satellite down **4.35 m** in the first second. If it moves sideways at **7.67 km/s**, after one second the sphere at that altitude has also curved down by exactly 4.35 m. The satellite keeps falling, but its height does not change: that is a circular orbit. The "fall equals curve" condition gives the circular-speed formula directly:

<Formula label="Circular speed and period" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^{3}}{\mu}}, \qquad r = R_E + h" where="
v :: horizontal speed needed for a circular orbit (km/s)
\mu :: Earth's gravitational parameter, 398,600.4 km³/s²
r :: distance to the Earth's centre: radius 6371 km plus altitude h
T :: period, the time for one revolution
" />

<Note kind="myth">

"There is no gravity in orbit, so astronauts float." **Wrong.** At ISS altitude gravity is still about 88 % of its surface value. Gravity is exactly what keeps the station in orbit. Everything on board falls freely with the same acceleration, so nothing presses on the floor: that is <Term id="ch01-microgravity">microgravity</Term>.

</Note>

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">A satellite does not "hover": it is in continuous free fall, moving sideways fast enough for the ground to curve away beneath it.</mark> The farther from the Earth, the weaker gravity, so the required speed is lower and the period longer. Chapter 2 computes orbits exactly; Chapter 3 computes how much propellant a rocket needs to reach that speed.

</Note>

## 1.2 The four segments of a satellite system

A satellite does not make maps or phone calls on its own. It is one link in a system of four **segments**:

- **Launch segment**: the rocket that puts the satellite into its initial orbit from a launch site. Many small satellites share one ride (<Term id="rideshare">rideshare</Term>). Chapter 3.
- **Space segment**: the satellite itself, made of the <Term id="satellite-bus">bus</Term> that keeps it alive (power, attitude, thermal, computer, communications) and the <Term id="payload">payload</Term> that does the job (camera, radar, transponder). Chapter 4.
- **Ground segment**: the control centre that sends commands and receives health data (<Term id="ttc">TT&C</Term>), the <Term id="ground-station">ground stations</Term> that receive data, and the processing, archiving and distribution systems. Communications systems also have <Term id="gateway-station">gateway stations</Term> linking the satellites to terrestrial networks. Chapter 5.
- **User segment**: the devices and people that use the service: the GNSS chip in a phone, a TV dish, a Starlink <Term id="user-terminal">user terminal</Term>, and the GIS engineer opening an image in Earth Engine.

Remote sensing usually follows the path **satellite → ground station → processing → user**: images must come down to a station and be processed before they become products. Navigation and communications differ: users receive signals **directly** from the satellites. A GNSS receiver only listens and never transmits, so one GPS system serves an unlimited number of users.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Segment | What it contains | Sentinel-2 example | Viet Nam example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Launch | Rockets, launch sites, rideshare services | Sentinel-2A launched on Vega from Kourou in 2015 | VNREDSat-1 shared Vega flight VV02 with Proba-V (7 May 2013) |
| Space | Bus + payload | The payload is the MultiSpectral Instrument (MSI), 13 bands | VNREDSat-1, about 130 kg, NAOMI camera 2.5 m / 10 m |
| Ground | Control (TT&C), receiving stations, processing, archiving | ESA's <Term id="ground-segment">ground segment</Term> produces L1C and L2A | S-band control station at Hoa Lac; Que Duong satellite control station (VINASAT) |
| User | People and software using the data or service | You, opening an image in Earth Engine or QGIS | Natural-resource, environment and agriculture agencies; satellite-TV subscribers |

</div>

<Note kind="why" label="Why do observation satellites store images before sending them?">

A satellite at 700 km "sees" a given ground station for at most about **12 minutes** per overhead pass (5° minimum elevation), and not every orbit passes near the station. Most of the time it images places with no station in view, so images are stored in on-board memory and "dumped" during a station pass. That is why space agencies build stations near the poles, where polar-orbiting satellites pass on almost every orbit.

</Note>

Users usually see only the last segment, but data quality depends on all four: the orbit is kept by the launcher and thrusters, the sensor is calibrated in orbit, and the ground segment decides which <Term id="data-level">processing level</Term> you download.

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">A failure in any segment breaks the service</mark>: a failed rocket loses the satellite, a failed bus makes the payload useless, and a ground-control fault leaves a healthy satellite with nobody able to command it. From 9 November 2021 to 25 March 2022 VNREDSat-1 stopped imaging for more than four months because of a hardware fault in its operational and control data storage; engineers had to restore the control servers before it could image again (Tuoi Tre, 2025).

</Note>

## 1.3 Mission families and their orbits

Satellites are grouped by what they do. Each job needs its own kind of orbit, because altitude decides how much of the Earth a satellite sees, how much detail it resolves and how long a signal takes.

Orbit regions are named by altitude: <Term id="leo">low Earth orbit (LEO)</Term> below 2,000 km, <Term id="ch01-meo">medium Earth orbit (MEO)</Term> from 2,000 km up to GEO, geostationary orbit GEO at 35,786 km, and <Term id="ch01-heo">highly elliptical orbits (HEO)</Term> with a very distant apogee.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Mission family | Typical orbit | Examples | Notable engineering point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communications | GEO; LEO | VINASAT-1/2, Intelsat; Starlink, OneWeb | GEO: 3 satellites cover almost the whole Earth (except the poles) but a signal up and down takes about 239 ms. LEO: low latency but thousands of satellites (Chapter 6) |
| Navigation | MEO | GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou | Broadcast time signals from atomic clocks; receivers only listen (Chapter 7) |
| Earth observation | Sun-synchronous LEO | Landsat, Sentinel, VNREDSat-1, PlanetScope | Optical, thermal, radar; large data volumes need X- or Ka-band downlinks (Chapter 5) |
| Meteorology | GEO and polar LEO | Himawari-9, GOES; NOAA-20/21, MetOp | GEO images the full disk continuously to track clouds; LEO measures in more detail and covers the poles |
| Science | Very varied | ISS, Hubble, GRACE-FO; JWST at L2 | Extreme stability and accuracy |

</div>

The **geostationary** orbit (<Term id="geostationary-orbit">GEO</Term>) is a special case: its period equals one sidereal day (23 h 56 min 4 s) and it lies in the equatorial plane, so the satellite stays fixed above one point on the ground. Put T = 86,164 s into the period formula of section 1.1:

<Formula label="Radius of the geostationary orbit" tex="r_{GEO} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{\mu\,T_{s}^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}} \approx 42\,164\ \text{km}, \qquad h_{GEO} = r_{GEO} - R_{eq} \approx 35\,786\ \text{km}" where="
T_{s} :: one sidereal day, 86,164.1 s: the time the Earth takes to turn exactly once
R_{eq} :: equatorial radius, 6378.137 km
" />

<Note kind="myth">

"A geostationary satellite stands still in space." **Wrong.** It moves at **3.07 km/s** on a circle of radius 42,164 km, exactly in step with the Earth's rotation, so it looks motionless from the ground. That is why TV dishes do not need to track it. VINASAT-1 holds 132° E and VINASAT-2 131.8° E.

</Note>

Figure 1.4 shows something important for remote-sensing people: **Earth-observation satellites are a small part** of today's sky. LEO is crowded by Internet constellations, while each GNSS needs only a few dozen satellites in MEO. The roughly 2,600 "not grouped" satellites include military and technology-demonstration satellites and many commercial ones (including Chinese imaging satellites such as Jilin-1 and Gaofen) that CelesTrak has not put in any purpose group.

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">The mission decides the orbit.</mark> Communications needs wide coverage or low latency (GEO, or dense LEO), navigation needs many satellites in view everywhere at once (MEO), Earth observation needs a close look with even lighting (sun-synchronous LEO), meteorology needs both a constant watch (GEO) and detail (polar LEO).

</Note>

All of them fly at 680–790 km with an inclination near 98°. This is a <Term id="sun-synchronous-orbit">sun-synchronous orbit</Term>: the satellite passes each place at the same local time, so illumination is similar from image to image. Chapter 2 explains what is special about 98°.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: Sentinel-2A's altitude from its orbital elements

CelesTrak gives Sentinel-2A's mean motion: n = 14.3081738 revolutions per day.

1. Period: T = 1440 min / 14.3081738 = **100.64 min**.
2. Kepler's third law: a = ∛(μ T² / 4π²), with μ = 398,600.4 km³/s² and T = 6038.5 s, gives a = **7167.1 km**.
3. Altitude above the 6378.1 km equatorial radius: h = 7167.1 − 6378.1 = **789.0 km**.
4. Velocity: v = √(μ / a) = **7.46 km/s**, about 26,900 km/h.

ESA quotes a mean altitude of 786 km for Sentinel-2. The 3 km difference comes from the reference radius (equatorial versus local).

</div>

VNREDSat-1 is operated by the Vietnam National Space Center (VNSC). The November 2026 elements show something interesting: its actual inclination of 97.89° is **below** the sun-synchronous value for its altitude (98.10°). By the J2 formula (Chapter 2) its orbit plane turns 0.961°/day instead of 0.986°/day. The plane lags the Sun, so the overpass time gets **earlier**, by about 36 minutes per year if uncorrected. This is an estimate from one element set; the handbook has no VNSC operations data to confirm it.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Terra is drifting too">

Terra (carrying MODIS) has an inclination of 97.93°, also below the sun-synchronous value (98.14°). NASA stopped maintaining Terra's orbit as the mission entered its final phase, so its overpass time is getting earlier. When you compare long MODIS Terra records, remember that the illumination is no longer as fixed as in the early years.

</Depth>

The curve has three clear parts. From 1957 to the late 1980s the Soviet Union and the US launched steadily, over 100 times a year. After the Soviet Union dissolved, launches declined to a low of **52 in 2005**. ## 1.5 The life cycle of a mission

Space agencies divide a satellite project into <Term id="ch01-mission-phase">phases</Term>, each closed by a review that decides whether to continue. NASA names them Pre-A, A to F; ESA uses 0, A to F with similar content. Knowing the phases helps you read announcements such as "the mission has passed its Critical Design Review" or "the satellite is in commissioning".

<Steps title="The phases of a mission (NASA, ESA)" items={[
  { tag: 'Pre-A / 0', t: 'Concept', d: 'User needs, mission options. Ends with a mission concept review (MCR at NASA, MDR at ESA).' },
  { tag: 'A', t: 'Feasibility', d: 'Choose a concept, estimate cost and risk, set system requirements (SRR).' },
  { tag: 'B', t: 'Preliminary design', d: 'Choose technologies, design each subsystem. Ends with the Preliminary Design Review (PDR).' },
  { tag: 'C', t: 'Detailed design', d: 'Detailed drawings, building and testing component models. Ends with the Critical Design Review (CDR).' },
  { tag: 'D', t: 'Build, integrate, test, launch', d: 'Assembly, integration and testing (AIT): vibration, thermal vacuum, electromagnetic compatibility. Flight readiness review, then launch.' },
  { tag: 'LEOP', t: 'Launch and in-orbit checkout', d: 'The first days after launch (LEOP), then weeks to months of calibration and checks (commissioning). NASA puts this at the end of phase D, ESA at the start of phase E.' },
  { tag: 'E', t: 'Operations', d: 'Routine operations, delivering data and services. Can be extended many times if the satellite stays healthy.' },
  { tag: 'F', t: 'Close-out', d: 'Passivation (venting propellant, disconnecting batteries), deorbiting to burn up in the atmosphere, or a boost to a graveyard orbit for GEO satellites.' },
]} />

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">Most of a mission's time and money is spent before launch</mark>; phase E is when users finally get data. "NewSpace" companies such as Planet or SpaceX shorten the cycle by mass-producing identical satellites and accepting more risk per spacecraft.

</Note>

<div class="worked">

#### Example: the life cycle of VNREDSat-1

1. **August 2010**: Viet Nam signs a contract with EADS Astrium (France) to build the satellite and the ground stations (eoPortal).
2. **7 May 2013**: launch on a Vega rocket, **33 months** after the contract, very fast for a government satellite because it used an existing platform.
3. **4 September 2013**: in-orbit checkout is over and control is handed over to Viet Nam.
4. Design life **5 years**, i.e. to 2018. In 2021 and at the end of 2023 the satellite stopped because of control-system faults; by May 2025 it was back to normal operations (Tuoi Tre, 2025).
5. In November 2026 it has been flying for **13.5 years**, nearly three times its design life: a typical extended phase E.

</div>

| A | System Requirements Review (SRR), Mission/System Definition Review (MDR/SDR) | Preliminary Requirements Review (PRR) |
| B | Preliminary Design Review (PDR) | System Requirements Review (SRR), PDR |

</Depth>

Viet Nam had a person in space very early: **Pham Tuan** flew on Soyuz 37 on 23 July 1980, the first Asian in space. Viet Nam's first satellite was the communications satellite **VINASAT-1** (2008), followed by **VINASAT-2** (2012). **VNREDSat-1** (2013) was the first Earth-observation satellite. MicroDragon re-entered the atmosphere on 1 October 2024. **LOTUSat-1**, an X-band synthetic-aperture radar satellite built by NEC (Japan) under a 2020 contract, is not yet in the catalogue: its launch has slipped several times, partly because of setbacks in the development of the Epsilon S rocket.

## 1.7 From satellites to remote sensing

From here on the handbook focuses on one mission family: **Earth observation**. <Term id="remote-sensing">Remote sensing</Term> means gathering information about an object **without touching it**. In this handbook the "object" is land, water, vegetation and cities; "without touching" means measuring electromagnetic energy from a satellite hundreds of kilometres away.

Following the classic presentation of the Canada Centre for Remote Sensing (CCRS), every remote-sensing system has seven stages, from energy source to application. There are two ways to get the energy:

- A <Term id="passive-sensor">passive sensor</Term> measures energy that is already there: reflected sunlight (Sentinel-2, Landsat) or thermal radiation emitted by the surface itself (Landsat's thermal bands).
- An <Term id="active-sensor">active sensor</Term> emits its own energy and records the echo. Sentinel-1 radar (Chapter 16) is an example: it images at night and through clouds.

Sunlight crosses the atmosphere **twice** (down and up), so optical images always carry atmospheric effects (Chapters 8 and 12). Thermal energy crosses it once, from the surface up. Radar also crosses it twice, but the atmosphere is almost transparent to microwaves.

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">Passive sensors measure energy that is already there; active sensors send their own and record the echo.</mark> That is why radar images at night and through clouds, which matters a lot in Viet Nam, where the rainy season can go months without a clean cloud-free optical image.

</Note>

## 1.8 How large is one scene?
