## 9.1 From image to map

A satellite image gives each pixel a list of reflectances. A land manager needs answers like "this is water, that is housing". <Term id="classification">Classification</Term> assigns every pixel to a class. There are two main approaches:

- **Supervised:** you show the computer a few example pixels of each class (<Term id="training-sample">training samples</Term>). It learns each class's spectral signature and labels the remaining pixels.
- **Unsupervised:** the computer groups similar pixels into clusters by itself. You name the clusters afterwards.

This chapter reuses the 12 February 2024 Sentinel-2 image of West Lake – Red River (Chapter 6) with 5 classes: water, trees, low vegetation (crops, grass, shrubs), built-up and bare. As a yardstick it uses the <Term id="worldcover">ESA WorldCover</Term> 2021 map of the same frame as "reference".

**Important:** WorldCover is itself an automatic classification, not ground truth. Every "correct" number in this chapter is strictly **agreement with WorldCover**. A real assessment needs reference data of higher quality than the map being checked, such as interpretation of very-high-resolution imagery or field visits (section 9.5).

Units: km². Both classifiers find **too few trees and too much low vegetation**. Street trees in Hanoi have small crowns, so a 10 m pixel often mixes a tree with roofs and roads (mixed pixels, Chapter 4); its spectrum looks more like low vegetation than like closed canopy. Bare is overestimated almost ninefold: metal roofs, construction sites and wet sand have spectra close to the river sandbars.

### Where do the two classifiers differ?

<Figure
  title="Figure 9.2 — Red – NIR feature space: the boundaries of two classifiers"
  try="Switch between 'Minimum distance' and 'Maximum likelihood'. How does the boundary between trees (green) and low vegetation (yellow) move? Why is the Built-up ellipse long and tilted?"
  source="2500 random pixels of the 12 Feb 2024 Sentinel-2 image, coloured by WorldCover. Means and 2σ ellipses from the suggested samples (3 × 3 pixels per point). Background: classifiers trained on the 2 bands B4 and B8 only (the Figure 9.1 map uses 6 bands)."
>
  <FeatureSpace client:visible lang="en" />
</Figure>

- <Term id="minimum-distance">Minimum distance</Term> remembers only the **centre** of each class. A pixel goes to the nearest centre, so the boundaries are straight perpendicular bisectors.
- <Term id="maximum-likelihood">Maximum likelihood</Term> remembers the centre and the **spread** (covariance matrix) of each class. A widely spread class like Built-up (bright metal roofs, tiles, dark asphalt) claims a larger region; a tight class like Water accepts only very similar pixels. The boundaries curve.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Formulas">

For a spectral vector **x**, class mean **μₖ** and covariance **Σₖ** (from the training samples):

<p class="formula">Minimum distance: k* = argminₖ ‖x − μₖ‖²</p>
<p class="formula">Maximum likelihood: k* = argmaxₖ [ −½ ln|Σₖ| − ½ (x − μₖ)ᵀ Σₖ⁻¹ (x − μₖ) ]</p>

Maximum likelihood needs at least d + 1 sample pixels per class (d = number of bands, here 6) for Σₖ to be invertible. The Bare class has only 5 points × 9 pixels, so the handbook adds a small ridge (0.1 % of the mean variance) to the diagonal of Σₖ. Random Forest (Breiman 2001) makes no normality assumption and usually beats both, but it cannot be drawn as simply in feature space; you will try it in the Earth Engine lab.

</Depth>

With k = 5 (run 1), one cluster covers 29 % of the frame, has a centre NDVI of −0.13 and is 94 % water in WorldCover: easy to name. But **three of the five clusters** are mostly built-up (88 %, 74 % and 59 %), differing in roof brightness. Trees get one cluster (NDVI 0.63, 74 % trees). Low vegetation and bare get none. The lesson: k-means groups by **spectrum**; people's classes group by **function**, and the two need not coincide.

## 9.4 Confusion matrix and the kinds of accuracy

To know how right a map is, take a **validation sample** of random pixels, **separate from the training samples**, and compare the map label with the reference label at each one. The counts form a <Term id="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</Term>: rows are map classes, columns are reference classes.

- <Term id="overall-accuracy">Overall accuracy (OA)</Term>: the share of correct pixels.
- <Term id="users-accuracy">User's accuracy (UA)</Term> of a class: of the pixels the map calls "trees", what share really are trees? The question of the map **user**.
- <Term id="producers-accuracy">Producer's accuracy (PA)</Term>: of the real trees, what share did the map find? The question of the map **maker**.
- **F1** is the harmonic mean of UA and PA.

With a stratified design of 50 points per class (sample 1):

- The raw OA (plain count over 250 points) is 60.8 %. The OA **weighted** by each class's map area is **72.5 ± 3.7 %**. Only the latter applies to the whole map: a plain count is biased because every class got 50 points whatever its size. The true agreement over all pixels is 70.5 %, inside the interval.
- UA of Water = 100 %, but UA of Bare is only 4 %: most pixels the map calls bare are really built-up (30/50) or water (13/50).
- Kappa = 0.51. Pontius & Millones (2011) showed that kappa's comparison with "random agreement" has no practical meaning; report OA, UA, PA and estimated areas instead.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: estimating the tree area from the sample

The map counts 2.54 km² of trees, but real trees also hide inside other map classes. The Trees (reference) column of the matrix tells how many of the 50 sample points of each map class are really trees:

1. Map area share of each class: Water W = 0.236; Trees 0.097; Low veg. 0.224; Built-up 0.382; Bare 0.062.
2. Share of tree points in each map class: 0/50, 43/50, 27/50, 2/50, 5/50.
3. Tree area share = 0.236 × 0 + 0.097 × 0.86 + 0.224 × 0.54 + 0.382 × 0.04 + 0.062 × 0.10 ≈ 0.226.
4. Area = 0.226 × 26.2 km² ≈ **5.91 km²**, 95 % confidence interval ± 1.02 km².

The WorldCover area, 6.19 km², lies inside 4.89–6.93 km². Pixel counting (2.54 km²) is off by more than a factor of two, while 250 sample points get close. This is why Olofsson et al. (2014) advise **never reporting areas by counting pixels**.

</div>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Olofsson et al. (2014) estimators">

<p class="formula">p̂ᵢⱼ = Wᵢ nᵢⱼ / nᵢ.     Â_j = A Σᵢ p̂ᵢⱼ     SE(p̂.ⱼ) = √ Σᵢ Wᵢ² (nᵢⱼ/nᵢ.)(1 − nᵢⱼ/nᵢ.) / (nᵢ. − 1)</p>
<p class="formula">OA = Σⱼ p̂ⱼⱼ     UAᵢ = p̂ᵢᵢ / p̂ᵢ.     PAⱼ = p̂ⱼⱼ / p̂.ⱼ     95 % CI = ± 1.96 A · SE</p>

A stratified design with a minimum number of points per class (often 50–100) gives rare classes like Bare enough samples. With a simple random sample of 250 points, Bare gets only about 15, so its UA and PA swing widely from sample to sample.

</Depth>

## 9.5 Reference data: the weakest link

An assessment is only as good as its reference data. ESA's validation report gives WorldCover v200 a global overall accuracy of about 77 %, so roughly one pixel in four may be wrong. When your map "disagrees with WorldCover", your map may be wrong, WorldCover may be wrong, or both. Moreover WorldCover is from 2021 and the image from 2024: land built on in those three years counts as an "error".

In real work:

1. Draw a stratified validation sample from your own map.
2. Interpret each point on very-high-resolution imagery of the same date (Google Earth, drone imagery) or in the field, and record your confidence.
3. Report the area-weighted confusion matrix, OA, UA, PA and areas with confidence intervals.
