## 12.1 From photons to numbers

Sunlight crosses the atmosphere, hits the ground, is reflected and crosses the atmosphere again before reaching the sensor. On the way the atmosphere both **adds** light (scattering into the view) and **removes** light (absorption, scattering out). Satellite data are therefore released at several <Term id="data-level">processing levels</Term>:

| Sentinel-2 level | Pixel value | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Level-1C | <Term id="toa-reflectance">Top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance</Term> × 10,000 | You need raw data, your own atmospheric correction, or cloud studies |
| Level-2A | <Term id="surface-reflectance">Surface reflectance</Term> × 10,000, plus the SCL layer | Most applications: indices, classification, time series |

Chapters 9, 11 and 16 use Level-2A. This chapter opens the black box to see how L2A is made:

Level-1C is top-of-atmosphere reflectance, Level-2A is surface reflectance. Most applications (indices, classification, time series) should use L2A.

</Note>

## 12.2 Radiometric calibration: from DN to radiance

Sentinel-2 L1C files store integers (DN). Since processing baseline 04.00 (2022), ESA adds 1000 to every value and records `RADIO_ADD_OFFSET = −1000` in the metadata. To undo it:

<Formula label="Digital number → top-of-atmosphere reflectance (Sentinel-2 L1C)" tex="\rho_{TOA} = \frac{DN + \mathrm{OFFSET}}{10\,000}" where="
DN :: integer value in the L1C file
\mathrm{OFFSET} :: RADIO_ADD_OFFSET in the metadata, −1000 from processing baseline 04.00
" />

To get the energy actually reaching the sensor, convert reflectance into <Term id="radiance">radiance</Term> L with the band's solar irradiance E, the solar zenith angle θs and the Earth–Sun distance factor U = 1/d²:

<Formula label="Reflectance → radiance" tex="L = \frac{\rho_{TOA} \cdot U \cdot E \cdot \cos\theta_s}{\pi}" where="
L :: radiance at the sensor (W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹·µm⁻¹)
U :: Earth–Sun distance factor, 1/d²
E :: band solar irradiance (W·m⁻²·µm⁻¹)
\theta_s :: solar zenith angle
" />

<div class="worked">

#### Example: the blue band over West Lake

Metadata of 12 Feb 2024: θs = 41.43°, U = 1.0280 (in February the Earth is close to the Sun, d ≈ 0.986 astronomical units), E(B2) = 1959.75 W·m⁻²·µm⁻¹.

1. DN in the original L1C file: 2210. Subtract 1000: 1210, so ρ_TOA = 0.121.
2. L = 0.121 × 1.028 × 1959.75 × cos 41.43° / π ≈ **58.2 W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹·µm⁻¹**.
3. After atmospheric correction (L2A) the surface reflectance is only **0.040**. <mark>About **two thirds** of the blue signal over the lake is light scattered by the atmosphere.</mark>

</div>

<Note kind="key">

The number in the file is not yet surface reflectance. The offset from the metadata must be removed, and over a dark surface such as a lake most of the blue signal comes from the atmosphere, not from the ground.

</Note>

## 12.3 What the atmosphere does to an image

Two main processes:

- **Scattering.** Gas molecules far smaller than the wavelength cause <Term id="rayleigh-scattering">Rayleigh scattering</Term>, whose strength scales with λ⁻⁴. Blue B2 (492 nm) is scattered (833/492)⁴ ≈ **8.2 times** more than near-infrared B8 (833 nm). <Term id="aerosol">Aerosols</Term> (dust, smoke) are larger, scatter with less wavelength dependence and cause haze.
- **Absorption.** Water vapour, ozone and CO₂ absorb in specific ranges. Sensors place their bands in atmospheric windows (Chapter 8) to avoid most of it, but some remains.

Averaged over the 5.12 × 5.12 km frame, TOA minus surface reflectance:

| Band | B2 blue | B3 green | B4 red | B8 NIR | B11 SWIR1 | B12 SWIR2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TOA − surface | +0.072 | +0.039 | +0.023 | −0.009 | −0.017 | −0.027 |

The effect shrinks from blue to red, as Rayleigh scattering predicts. In NIR and SWIR the difference is **negative**: the atmosphere absorbs some light, so correction **raises** the surface reflectance. At the floodplain-vegetation point B8 rises from 0.316 (TOA) to 0.431 (surface).

<Note kind="key">

The atmosphere brightens the blue band (scattering) and darkens NIR and SWIR (absorption). In practice NDVI from TOA images is lower than from surface reflectance, and the gap changes from day to day with the haze. To compare dates, use L2A.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="How does atmospheric correction work?">

<Term id="atmospheric-correction">Atmospheric correction</Term> needs the state of the atmosphere at acquisition time: water vapour, aerosol optical thickness, pressure. Sen2Cor (the processor that makes Sentinel-2 L2A) estimates them **from the image itself**: water vapour from band B9 (945 nm), aerosols from dark pixels such as dense forest and deep water. It then uses look-up tables from a radiative-transfer model to turn TOA into surface reflectance.

The simplest method is **dark-object subtraction** (Chavez, 1988): assume the darkest pixel in each band has near-zero reflectance, so its value is the atmospheric contribution. It corrects scattering only, not absorption.

</Depth>

## 12.4 Clouds and cloud shadows

Hanoi is very cloudy (Chapter 9: only 92 of 433 acquisition dates were clear enough). Most images are partly cloudy, so cloud and shadow pixels must be **masked** before any computation. Two common tools for Sentinel-2:

- <Term id="cloud-score-plus">Cloud Score+</Term>: scores each pixel from 0 (cloud or shadow) to 1 (clear). The `cs_cdf` band is recommended with a threshold around 0.5–0.65.
- <Term id="scl">SCL</Term>: Sen2Cor's classification. Usually classes 3 (cloud shadow), 8, 9 (cloud) and 10 (thin cirrus) are masked.

<Note kind="key">

No mask is perfect: a strict threshold misses fewer clouds but loses data, a loose one does the opposite. State the mask and threshold you used.

</Note>

<div class="worked">

#### Example: a shadow tells the cloud's height

On 5 Oct 2023 the Sun stood at azimuth 146.8° (south-east) and zenith angle 30.2°, so shadows fall to the **north-west**. In the SCL, the largest cloud is centred at row 313, column 343; the large shadow next to it is centred at row 235, column 261.

1. Distance between the centres: √(78² + 82²) × 10 m ≈ **1130 m**.
2. Cloud height: h = 1130 / tan 30.2° ≈ **1.9 km**.

This is a rough estimate (cloud and shadow outlines do not match exactly), but it is within the usual height range of cumulus. Shadow-detection algorithms use exactly this geometry: they project clouds away from the Sun at a series of trial heights.

</div>

## 12.5 Cloud-free composites

When no single day is clear, combine many. The common choice is a <Term id="median-composite">median composite</Term>: at each pixel, take the median of all acquisitions in a period.

<Note kind="myth">

"A median composite removes clouds by itself." Only when **more than half** of the looks at that pixel are clear. In cloudy regions, mask clouds first and take the median afterwards.

</Note>

The 2023 rainy season in Hanoi was not like that: the unmasked median still leaves **66 %** of pixels cloud-bright (mean of the three visible bands > 0.2). Mask first and then take the median, and only **0.8 %** remain. Each pixel keeps between 2 and 11 clear looks (median 7) out of 19.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Median, mean or medoid?">

- The **mean** is pulled up by even one leftover cloud.
- The **per-band median** resists outliers, but each band may come from a different date, creating spectra that never existed.
- The **medoid** (Flood, 2013) picks the whole acquisition closest to the multi-dimensional median, so it keeps the real spectrum of one date.

For time series (Chapter 15), do not composite over too long a period: mixing dry and wet seasons blends two states of rice and water.

</Depth>
