## 5.1 Radio waves and frequency bands

All communication with satellites travels as electromagnetic waves at the speed of light. Wavelength and frequency are linked by:

<Formula label="Wavelength" tex="\lambda = \frac{c}{f}, \qquad c = 299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s}" where="
\lambda :: wavelength (m)
f :: frequency (Hz)
" />

At 437 MHz (a CubeSat UHF radio) λ = 68.6 cm; at 1.575 GHz (GPS L1) 19.0 cm; at 2.2 GHz 13.6 cm; at 8.1 GHz 3.7 cm; at 12 GHz 2.5 cm; at 30 GHz only 1.0 cm. Engineers name frequency ranges with letters (<Term id="frequency-band">bands</Term>, per IEEE Std 521), and each band has its own "personality":

| Band | Frequency (GHz) | Wavelength | Used for | In rain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UHF, L | 0.3–2 | 15–100 cm | Small satellites, GNSS (Chapter 7), mobile satellite, L-band SAR | Almost unaffected |
| S | 2–4 | 7.5–15 cm | Satellite control (TT&C), S-band SAR | Negligible |
| C | 4–8 | 3.8–7.5 cm | TV and trunking via geostationary satellites in rainy regions; Sentinel-1 radar | Very small |
| X | 8–12 | 2.5–3.8 cm | Earth-observation image downlink (8025–8400 MHz), X-band radar | Small |
| Ku | 12–18 | 1.7–2.5 cm | Direct-to-home TV, VSAT, satellite-internet terminals | A few dB to over 10 dB in the tropics |
| Ka | 27–40 | 0.75–1.1 cm | High-throughput satellites, gateways | Very large |
| V, W | 40–110 | 2.7–7.5 mm | Next-generation gateways (E-band 71–86 GHz), inter-satellite links | Very large, water vapour too |
| Optical | ≈ 194,000 (1550 nm) | µm | Inter-satellite lasers (EDRS), optical downlink | Blocked completely by cloud |

The general rule: **the higher the frequency, the more bandwidth** (so the more Mbit/s), the more a given antenna concentrates, but **the more it fears rain**. Viet Nam's VINASAT-1 (geostationary at 132°E) carries 8 C-band and 12 Ku-band transponders; VINASAT-2 (131.8°E) carries Ku-band.

In satellite communications people talk about specific sub-bands rather than IEEE boundaries. Commercial "Ku" is a 10.7–12.75 GHz downlink and a 13.75–14.5 GHz uplink; commercial "Ka" is a 17.7–20.2 GHz downlink and a 27.5–30 GHz uplink. **The uplink usually takes the higher band.** The satellite is short of power, so it gets the lower band, where rain and gases attenuate less and on-board amplifiers are more efficient; the ground station has power to spare and takes the higher band.

## 5.2 The language of decibels

Satellite signals meet huge and tiny factors: an antenna amplifies by tens of thousands, the path weakens the signal by 10²⁰. Written in <Term id="decibel">decibels</Term>, multiplication becomes addition:

<Formula label="Decibels" tex="x_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_2}{P_1}, \qquad P_{\text{dBW}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P}{1\ \text{W}}, \qquad P_{\text{dBm}} = P_{\text{dBW}} + 30" where="
P_1,\ P_2 :: the two powers being compared
P_{\text{dBW}} :: power relative to 1 W
P_{\text{dBm}} :: power relative to 1 mW
" />

| Unit | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| dB | Power ratio | +3 dB ≈ ×2; +10 dB = ×10; −20 dB = ×1/100 |
| dBW, dBm | Power relative to 1 W or 1 mW | 100 W = 20 dBW = 50 dBm; 1 mW = −30 dBW |
| dBi | Antenna gain relative to an isotropic antenna | 0.6 m dish at 12 GHz: 35.3 dBi |
| dBK, dB/K | Noise temperature; the G/T ratio | 150 K = 21.8 dBK |
| dB·Hz | Bandwidth or C/N0 | 1 MHz = 60.0 dB·Hz; 36 MHz = 75.6 dB·Hz |

Mental trick: split a dB value into **tens** (each 10 dB is one zero) and a **remainder** (3 dB ≈ ×2, 6 dB ≈ ×4, 7 dB ≈ ×5). 33 dB = 30 + 3 dB = ×1000 × 2 = ×2000.

<Note kind="myth">

"Two 10 dBW transmitters add up to 20 dBW." Wrong: adding dB **multiplies** powers. Two 10 W transmitters give 20 W, which is 13 dBW (3 dB more). Add dB only when one quantity passes through a chain of gains or losses in series.

</Note>

<Note kind="key">

A radio link is **one sum in dB**: transmit power + antenna gain − losses + receive antenna gain − noise. Remember this sentence and you can read any link-budget table.

</Note>

## 5.3 Antennas: pushing energy one way

An isotropic (ideal) antenna radiates equally in all directions. Real antennas squeeze the energy into a narrow beam. A parabolic dish collects waves like a concave mirror collects light. The <Term id="antenna-gain">gain</Term> and half-power beamwidth of a dish of diameter D:

<Formula label="Gain and beamwidth of a parabolic dish" tex="G = \eta\left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2, \qquad \theta_{3\text{dB}} \approx 70\,\frac{\lambda}{D}\ (^\circ)" where="
G :: gain (linear); in dBi it is 10 log10 G
\eta :: aperture efficiency, typically 0.55–0.7
D :: dish diameter (m)
\theta_{3\text{dB}} :: beamwidth at which power falls to half
" />

For a given dish, gain grows with the square of frequency and the beam narrows accordingly. That is why a Ku-band dish is smaller than a C-band dish for the same performance, but must be pointed more precisely. A 0.6 m home TV dish at 12 GHz gives 35.3 dBi with a 2.9° beam; a 5.4 m image-reception dish at 8.1 GHz gives 51.0 dBi with a beam of only 0.48°; a 13 m dish gives 58.6 dBi.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: a 1.2 m Ku-band dish

1. λ at 12 GHz = 2.5 cm. G = 0.6 × (π × 1.2 / 0.025)² ≈ 13,700, i.e. **41.4 dBi**.
2. Beamwidth ≈ 70 × 0.025 / 1.2 = **1.46°**.
3. If the dish is off by 0.73° (half the beam), the signal has already dropped **3 dB**, half the power. Wind shaking the dish by a few tenths of a degree is enough to notice.

</div>

On the transmit side the key quantity is <Term id="eirp">EIRP</Term> (equivalent isotropically radiated power), the "brightness" of the transmitter towards the receiver:

<Formula label="EIRP" tex="\mathrm{EIRP}_{\text{dBW}} = P_t\,[\text{dBW}] + G_t\,[\text{dBi}]" where="
P_t :: power fed to the transmit antenna
G_t :: transmit antenna gain
" />

A 10 W satellite with a 6 dBi antenna has an EIRP of 16 dBW, like an isotropic antenna radiating 40 W in the main direction. The Ku-band TV scenario of Figure 5.1 uses an EIRP of 52 dBW (illustrative value).

### Phased arrays

Instead of a mechanically steered dish, a <Term id="signals-phased-array">phased-array antenna</Term> has hundreds to thousands of small elements radiating the same signal with slightly different phases. The waves add up in the wanted direction and cancel elsewhere. To steer the beam by θ₀ from broadside, two neighbouring elements spaced d apart need a phase step:

<Formula label="Steering a phased array" tex="\Delta\varphi = \frac{2\pi d \sin\theta_0}{\lambda}" where="
d :: spacing between two elements
\theta_0 :: steering angle from the panel normal
" />

- **Element spacing is usually ≤ λ/2.** Wider, and other directions also see the elements in phase: <Term id="signals-grating-lobe">grating lobes</Term> as strong as the main beam send energy to the wrong place. At 12 GHz, λ/2 = 1.25 cm.
- **The beam moves electronically in microseconds**, with no moving parts, and several beams can be formed at once.
- **Drawbacks:** the further from broadside, the more the effective area shrinks with cos θ₀ (3.0 dB lost at 60°) and the beam doubles in width (with 16 elements: 6.3° at broadside, 12.7° at 60°). Phased arrays are also power-hungry and expensive; cheap integrated beamformer chips are what brought them to consumer terminals.

### Polarisation

A wave can be **linearly polarised** (horizontal or vertical) or **circularly polarised** (right-hand RHCP, left-hand LHCP). Two orthogonal polarisations let the same frequency be used twice. At low frequencies (L, S) the ionosphere rotates the plane of a linear wave (Faraday rotation), so GNSS uses RHCP: the receiver does not need to know how far the wave turned. Ku-band TV usually uses linear polarisation, and the installer must rotate the receiver head (LNB) to match. At Ku and Ka, large raindrops flatten as they fall and **depolarise** the wave: some energy leaks into the other polarisation and interferes with the channel sharing that frequency.

<Note kind="key">

An antenna creates no energy; it only **pushes energy in one direction**. A larger dish or a higher frequency gives a narrower beam and more gain, at the price of more precise pointing; a phased array steers that beam with phase instead of motors.

</Note>

## 5.4 Free-space path loss

Leaving the antenna, energy spreads over ever larger spheres and its density falls as 1/d². Computed for an isotropic receive antenna (effective area λ²/4π), this gives the <Term id="fspl">free-space path loss</Term>:

<Formula label="Free-space path loss (FSPL)" tex="L_{fs} = \left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; L_{fs}\,[\text{dB}] = 20\log_{10} d_{\text{km}} + 20\log_{10} f_{\text{GHz}} + 92.45" where="
d :: satellite–station distance
f :: frequency
" />

  why="FSPL = 20·log10(4π × 36,975 km × 12 GHz / c) = 205.4 dB: the signal weakens about 3.5 × 10²⁰ times. That is why a TV satellite needs an EIRP around 50 dBW while home dishes stay small." />

For a low-orbit satellite, distance changes throughout a pass. Sentinel-2 flies at 786 km: at zenith the X-band (8.1 GHz) FSPL is 168.5 dB; at 5° elevation the <Term id="slant-range">slant range</Term> is 2,754 km and FSPL 179.4 dB. Almost 11 dB of difference within minutes, so Earth-observation satellites often use "isoflux" antennas that radiate more towards the edge of coverage, or change the data rate with elevation.

<Note kind="myth">

"Higher frequencies lose more in vacuum." FSPL grows with f² only because of the convention of an isotropic receive antenna, whose collecting area shrinks at higher frequency. Keep **the dish sizes fixed** at both ends and each dish gains 6 dB when the frequency doubles, more than offsetting the 6 dB of extra loss. In vacuum, high-frequency waves do not "lose" more energy; the real drawback of high frequencies is rain and the atmosphere.

</Note>

<Note kind="key">

Doubling the distance or doubling the frequency each adds **6 dB** of loss. The path to a geostationary satellite costs about 200 dB, the path to a low-orbit satellite about 170–180 dB.

</Note>

## 5.5 Thermal noise and G/T

Everything at temperature T radiates thermal noise. The noise power a receiver "hears" in bandwidth B:

<Formula label="Thermal noise" tex="N = k\,T_{sys}\,B, \qquad k = 1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K} = -228.6\ \text{dBW/(K}\cdot\text{Hz)}" where="
k :: Boltzmann's constant
T_{sys} :: system noise temperature (K)
B :: bandwidth (Hz)
" />

The <Term id="system-noise-temperature">system noise temperature</Term> has several parts: sky noise seen by the antenna (a clear Ku-band sky is only a few tens of K, but the warm ground at about 290 K leaks into the sidelobes), cable losses, and the noise of the low-noise amplifier (LNA, or the LNB of a TV dish). By the Friis formula, the first amplifier stage dominates:

<Formula label="Friis formula for a receiver chain" tex="T_{sys} = T_{ant} + T_1 + \frac{T_2}{G_1} + \frac{T_3}{G_1 G_2} + \cdots" where="
T_{ant} :: antenna noise temperature (sky, ground, rain)
T_1,\ G_1 :: noise temperature and gain of the first stage (LNA)
T_2,\ T_3 :: later stages: divided by the gain in front of them
" />

That is why the LNB sits right at the dish focus: a long cable before the LNA would add its loss and noise straight into T_sys. The quality of a receiving station boils down to one number, <Term id="g-over-t">G/T</Term>:

<Formula label="Receiving-station figure of merit" tex="\frac{G}{T}\,[\text{dB/K}] = G_r\,[\text{dBi}] - 10\log_{10} T_{sys}" where="
G_r :: receive antenna gain
T_{sys} :: system noise temperature
" />

<Note kind="key">

A weak signal is less of a problem than a **signal weak compared with the noise**. A good station has a high G/T: a big dish to collect the signal, a cold LNA right at the focus to keep the noise down.

</Note>

## 5.6 The link budget

Putting it all together, the carrier-to-noise-density ratio at the receiver is:

<Formula label="The link equation" tex="\frac{C}{N_0}\,[\text{dB}\cdot\text{Hz}] = \mathrm{EIRP} - L_{fs} - L_{rain} - L_{other} + \frac{G}{T} + 228.6" where="
L_{rain} :: rain attenuation
L_{other} :: gases, pointing, polarisation mismatch, equipment
" />

<Formula label="From C/N0 to link margin" tex="\frac{E_b}{N_0} = \frac{C}{N_0} - 10\log_{10} R_b, \qquad \frac{E_s}{N_0} = \frac{C}{N_0} - 10\log_{10} R_s, \qquad M = \left(\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right)_{have} - \left(\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right)_{need}" where="
R_b :: bit rate (bit/s)
R_s :: symbol rate (symbol/s); occupied bandwidth B = R_s(1 + α), α = 0.05–0.35
M :: link margin (dB)
" />

<Term id="cn0">C/N0</Term> says how strong the signal is relative to noise. <Term id="ebn0">Eb/N0</Term> divides that energy among the bits: sending 10 times faster leaves each bit 10 dB weaker. Every modulation and coding scheme needs a minimum Eb/N0 (section 5.7). What is left over is the <Term id="link-margin">link margin</Term>, kept for rain, equipment ageing, and dish mispointing.

### Rain, atmosphere and availability

Above about 10 GHz, raindrops (a few mm across, comparable to the wavelength) absorb and scatter the wave. ITU-R P.838 gives the specific attenuation γ = k·R^α (dB/km) for rain rate R (mm/h); P.618 integrates it along the slant path through the rain layer, as the value exceeded for p % of an average year. From the ITU-R P.837-7 map, the rain rate exceeded 0.01 % of the time (R0.01, about 53 minutes per year) is **88.8 mm/h in Hanoi** and 89.5 mm/h in Ho Chi Minh City, against 9.4 mm/h at Svalbard. At 12 GHz Hanoi's R0.01 gives γ = 4.2 dB/km, and the rain layer is about 5 km thick.

Rain hurts a second time: the warm water the antenna looks through **radiates noise** and raises the system temperature:

<Formula label="Rain noise" tex="\Delta T = T_m\left(1 - 10^{-A/10}\right), \qquad T_m \approx 275\ \text{K}" where="
A :: rain attenuation (dB)
T_m :: mean temperature of the rain layer
" />

A 3 dB fade adds 137 K to T_sys; with a 120 K dish the noise almost doubles. That is why a storm pulls Es/N0 down by more than the fade itself.

Other effects:

- **Gases:** water vapour absorbs near 22 GHz, oxygen near 60 GHz (ITU-R P.676). That makes 60 GHz good for links between satellites: the ground cannot hear them, and they cannot be jammed from below.
- **Ionosphere:** delay and scintillation at L and S band, strongest near the magnetic equator after sunset in solar-maximum years. Viet Nam lies in this zone; Chapter 7 covers the effect on GNSS.
- **Availability:** a link designed for 99.9 % of the year may be down about 8.8 hours a year; 99.99 % allows only about 53 minutes. At Ku-band in Hanoi that step costs about 8 dB more margin (5.1 → 13.0 dB).
- **Remedies:** power margin; adaptive coding and modulation (ACM, section 5.7); gateway site diversity (two stations tens of km apart rarely see heavy rain together); uplink power control.

<Note kind="key">

In Viet Nam **C-band is almost immune to rain**, Ku-band needs about 5 dB of margin for 99.9 % of the time, and Ka-band can lose the signal completely in a downpour. That is why C-band is still widely used for trunking and professional TV, while Ku/Ka systems rely on ACM and several gateways.

</Note>

## 5.7 Modulation, coding and the Shannon limit

<Term id="signals-modulation">Digital modulation</Term> maps each group of bits onto a "point" (symbol) in the phase–amplitude plane, called a constellation:

| Modulation | Bits/symbol | Character | Used in |
|---|---|---|---|
| BPSK | 1 | Two phases 0°/180°, the most robust | GNSS, TT&C, very weak links |
| QPSK | 2 | Four phases; same bit error rate as BPSK at the same Eb/N0 but twice the spectral efficiency | The workhorse of satellites |
| 8PSK | 3 | Eight phases, constant amplitude | DVB-S2 TV |
| 16APSK, 32APSK | 4, 5 | Concentric rings, less sensitive than QAM to satellite amplifier distortion | DVB-S2 on good links |
| 16–256QAM | 4–8 | Square grid, most efficient but needs high SNR and a linear amplifier | Terrestrial; some satellite links |

Without coding, QPSK needs Eb/N0 = 9.6 dB to make only 1 bit error in 100,000. <Term id="signals-fec">Forward error correction</Term> (FEC) adds structured redundant bits so the receiver can fix errors: code rate 3/4 means 3 data bits in every 4 bits sent. Generations of codes: convolutional + Reed–Solomon (DVB-S, older CCSDS), turbo (CCSDS, 3G), LDPC (DVB-S2, 5G, newer CCSDS). With LDPC, DVB-S2 QPSK 3/4 needs only Eb/N0 = 2.31 dB, 7 dB less than uncoded.

The ceiling for any code is the <Term id="shannon-limit">Shannon limit</Term>:

<Formula label="Shannon channel capacity" tex="C = B\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right), \qquad \frac{E_b}{N_0} \ge \frac{2^{\eta} - 1}{\eta} \;\xrightarrow{\;\eta\to 0\;}\; \ln 2 = -1.59\ \text{dB}" where="
C :: highest rate that can be sent reliably (bit/s)
B :: bandwidth (Hz)
S/N :: signal-to-noise ratio
\eta :: spectral efficiency C/B (bit/s/Hz)
" />

Two ways to grow capacity: more bandwidth B (linear gain) or more S/N (only logarithmic). That is why new systems race up to Ka, V, E-band and lasers for bandwidth, while using narrow beams for high S/N and reusing frequencies many times.

<Note kind="myth">

"Double the power, double the speed." By Shannon, on a link that is already strong, doubling the power (+3 dB) adds only about 1 bit/s/Hz. Doubling the **bandwidth** is what nearly doubles capacity. Only on very weak links (Eb/N0 near −1.6 dB) is rate roughly proportional to power.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="The −228.6 constant, the −1.59 dB limit and ACM">

N0 = k·T with k = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K, so 10·log10(k) = −228.6 dBW/(K·Hz). That is why "+228.6" appears in every link budget.

Capacity of a white-noise channel of bandwidth B: C = B·log2(1 + C/(N0·B)). Let B → ∞: C → (C/N0)/ln 2. Setting the rate Rb to that capacity gives Eb/N0 = (C/N0)/Rb = ln 2 = 0.693, i.e. −1.59 dB. Below this no code transmits reliably, however slowly. At 1 bit/s/Hz the limit is 0.00 dB, at 2 bit/s/Hz 1.76 dB, at 4 bit/s/Hz 5.74 dB.

Measured on DVB-S2 Table 13: QPSK 1/2 sits 1.07 dB from the Shannon limit, QPSK 3/4 1.47 dB, 8PSK 3/4 2.25 dB, 32APSK 9/10 2.85 dB. LDPC brings the low-efficiency modes very close to the theoretical ceiling.

**ACM in DVB-S2:** each data frame can carry a different MODCOD. The receiver reports its SNR; the gateway picks the mode for each terminal. When one customer is under rain, only that customer's frames switch to a more robust mode; everyone else keeps the fast mode.

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

Denser modulations carry more bits per symbol but need a stronger signal. Modern LDPC codes bring systems within 1–3 dB of the Shannon limit; the rest of the capacity race is about **more bandwidth**.

</Note>

## 5.8 Multiple access and frequency reuse

One satellite transponder serves many users at once. <Term id="signals-multiple-access">Multiple access</Term> is how they share the time–frequency plane:

- **FDMA:** one frequency slot per user, transmitting continuously. Simple, but guard bands between slots are wasted.
- **TDMA:** the whole band, taking turns in time slots. **MF-TDMA** combines both and is common in VSAT networks.
- **CDMA:** everyone on the same frequency at the same time, each with its own spreading code. GNSS works this way: every GPS satellite has its own code (section 5.13).
- **OFDMA:** the band is split into many orthogonal subcarriers, assigned to users in blocks (4G, 5G). Humphreys' group (University of Texas) showed in 2023 that the Starlink downlink uses OFDM in channels about 240 MHz wide.

<Figure
  title="Figure 5.9 — Five ways to share a satellite channel among users"

  source="Conceptual diagram; the assignments are illustrative. Starlink OFDM: Humphreys, T. E. et al. (2023), Signal Structure of the Starlink Ku-Band Downlink, IEEE TAES."
>
  <SignalAccess client:visible lang="en" />
</Figure>

Instead of one beam covering a whole country, modern satellites form tens to hundreds of <Term id="signals-spot-beam">spot beams</Term>. Beams that do not touch reuse the same frequencies: that is <Term id="signals-frequency-reuse">frequency reuse</Term>. The common "4-colour" scheme is 2 sub-bands × 2 polarisations. High-throughput satellites (HTS) use hundreds of such Ka-band beams. A few more techniques:

- **Beam hopping:** beams jump between cells on demand, concentrating capacity where the users are.
- **Bent-pipe and regenerative:** a bent-pipe satellite only shifts frequency and amplifies (ground → satellite → ground). A regenerative satellite demodulates, routes packets and remodulates; that is required to forward packets over inter-satellite lasers (Chapter 6).

A modern satellite's capacity comes not from one strong beam but from **many narrow beams reusing the same spectrum**. With 63 beams and a 4-colour scheme, the same 1 GHz of spectrum is used about 16 times over.

## 5.11 Earth-observation downlink and optical communication

Images are downlinked at X-band 8025–8400 MHz, the ITU band for the Earth exploration-satellite service. Landsat 8 packs 384 Mbit/s into 375 MHz of X-band with a rate-7/8 LDPC code (USGS). ## 5.12 TT&C, CCSDS and security

An Earth-observation satellite has two very different kinds of link:

- <Term id="ttc">TT&C</Term> (telemetry, tracking and command): commands up, housekeeping data down, ranging to determine the orbit. Low rates (a few kbit/s to a few Mbit/s), usually at S-band (2025–2110 MHz up, 2200–2290 MHz down), with near-omnidirectional antennas so the link works whatever the satellite's attitude. The 'LEO S-band' scenario of Figure 5.5 has 18.6 dB of margin: TT&C is designed with plenty to spare, because losing commands can mean losing the satellite.
- **Payload data**: bulk imagery at hundreds of Mbit/s, at X-band (section 5.11).

So that one station can receive satellites of many nations, space agencies share the <Term id="ccsds">CCSDS</Term> standards: data framing (TM, AOS), error-correcting codes (convolutional, Reed–Solomon, turbo, LDPC), file transfer and ranging protocols. Sentinel-2 packs its images in CCSDS AOS frames; that lets commercial stations receive many missions with one modem.

**Security.** The uplink is the weak point: anyone who can transmit on the right frequency in the right format can send commands. So commands are **authenticated** (the CCSDS SDLS standard at the link layer), and military and high-end commercial data are **encrypted** (AES) on board. Two other threats: **jamming** (high power drowning the signal) and **spoofing** (transmitting fake signals that look real). GNSS signals are weak and vulnerable to both (section 5.13, Chapter 7).

<Note kind="warn">

Losing the TT&C link means losing control of the satellite. Every serious system has backup stations, an automatic safe mode on board, and command authentication.

</Note>

## 5.13 GNSS signals: it is all about time

A GNSS satellite carries atomic clocks and keeps broadcasting "my time is t, my position is (x, y, z)". The receiver measures the travel time, from 67.4 ms (satellite at zenith, 20,200 km) to 86.1 ms (at the horizon, 25,801 km), to infer the distance. 1 ns of timing error is 30 cm of range error. Because the receiver clock is not accurate, at least 4 satellites are needed to solve 4 unknowns: x, y, z and the receiver clock error. Chapter 7 covers the solution, the error sources and the relativistic corrections.

The surprise is in the link budget: a GPS signal at the ground is **weaker than the noise**.

<Stats items={[
  { v: '−158.5', u: 'dBW', l: 'minimum GPS L1 C/A power at the ground (IS-GPS-200)' },
  { v: '−144.4', u: 'dBW', l: 'thermal noise at 130 K in 2.046 MHz' },
  { v: '43.1', u: 'dB', l: 'processing gain from spreading' },
]} caption="The signal sits 14 dB below the noise, but correlating with the known code recovers 43 dB." />

<Formula label="Processing gain of a spread-spectrum signal" tex="G_p = 10\log_{10}\frac{R_c}{R_b} = 10\log_{10}\frac{1.023\times10^6}{50} = 43.1\ \text{dB}" where="
R_c :: chip rate of the C/A code (1.023 Mchip/s)
R_b :: navigation data rate (50 bit/s)
" />

The 50 bit/s data are multiplied by a 1.023 Mchip/s pseudo-random code unique to each satellite (this is CDMA, section 5.8). The receiver knows the code in advance and correlates to gather the energy back. As a density: C/N0 = −158.5 − (−228.6 + 10·log10 130) = 49.0 dB·Hz, enough for the receiver to track. Because the signal is that weak, a jammer of a few watts can knock out GNSS over many kilometres.

<Note kind="key">

GNSS is a **timing** problem, and its signal lies below the noise. Spread spectrum (CDMA) turns what looks impossible into positioning in your pocket.

</Note>
