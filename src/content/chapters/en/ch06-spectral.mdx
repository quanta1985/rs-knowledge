## 11.1 What is spectral reflectance?

A leaf looks green because it absorbs most red and blue light and sends back relatively more green. The fraction of incoming energy a surface sends back, at each wavelength, is its <Term id="reflectance">spectral reflectance</Term>. Plotted against wavelength it gives a <Term id="spectral-signature">spectral signature</Term>, a kind of fingerprint of the surface.

Our eyes see roughly 400–700 nm. Sentinel-2 also measures the <Term id="red-edge">red edge</Term>, <Term id="nir">near infrared (NIR)</Term> and <Term id="swir">shortwave infrared (SWIR)</Term>. These "invisible" regions are what separate surfaces that look alike to the eye.

Reading the six reference curves in Figure 11.1:

- **Water** (West Lake, Red River): low in every band and near zero in NIR and SWIR, because water strongly absorbs infrared. The Red River is brighter than West Lake in green (0.082 versus 0.048).
- **Vegetation** (floodplain, urban trees): low in blue and red (chlorophyll absorption), a small green peak, then a **sharp rise** through the red edge into NIR. The floodplain pixel reaches 0.431 in B8, 13 times its red reflectance (0.033).
- **Built-up land and bare soil**: flatter curves rising towards SWIR. Bare soil is brightest in SWIR (0.33–0.34 in B11–B12).

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why do the curves look like this?">

- **Chlorophyll a and b** absorb strongly around 430–450 nm and 640–680 nm. These are the blue and red "valleys" of vegetation.
- The **spongy mesophyll** inside leaves has many air–cell interfaces that scatter NIR strongly. NIR is therefore high, and it rises with more leaf layers.
- **Leaf water** absorbs in bands around 1450 nm and 1940 nm, lowering SWIR reflectance. Dry or wilted vegetation has higher SWIR.
- **Surface water** reflectance comes mainly from suspended matter and algae in the water column. The Red River reflects more in green–red than West Lake, consistent with more suspended material. West Lake has higher NIR (0.031) than the Red River (0.022), possibly from algae; water-quality data would be needed to confirm.
- **Mixed pixels**: a 10 m pixel on a lake or river bank holds both water and trees, so its spectrum is intermediate (a <Term id="mixed-pixel">mixed pixel</Term>). Reference points were therefore taken in homogeneous areas, with a 3×3-pixel standard deviation below 0.012.
- The 20 m bands (B5, B6, B7, B8A, B11, B12) were resampled to 10 m by **nearest neighbour**, so they add no spatial detail.

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

Every surface has its own spectral curve. Water is dark in NIR and SWIR, vegetation jumps from red to NIR, bare soil brightens towards SWIR: the clearest differences lie where the eye cannot see.

</Note>

## 11.2 Sensor bands

A sensor does not measure a continuous curve. It averages over **wavelength intervals** called <Term id="band">spectral bands</Term>. Sentinel-2 has 13 bands; Landsat 8/9 OLI has 9. Choose "Band positions" in Figure 11.1 to see Sentinel-2's bands crowd into the red edge, where Landsat has none.

An image records the spectral curve only where its bands sit. Band position decides what you can see: Sentinel-2 has three red-edge bands, Landsat 8/9 has none there.

</Note>

## 11.3 Colour composites: seeing the invisible

A screen has only three colours, red, green and blue. A <Term id="composite">colour composite</Term> assigns any three spectral bands to them. Put NIR into red and vegetation turns bright red, because NIR is where vegetation is brightest.

## 11.4 Normalized-difference indices

Instead of looking at bands one by one, we combine two "contrasting" bands into one number:

<Formula label="Normalized-difference index" tex="I = \frac{A - B}{A + B}" where="
I :: index value, from −1 to 1
A :: band where the target surface is bright
B :: band where it is dark
" />

A is a band where the target surface is bright, B a band where it is dark. The result always lies between −1 and 1. Dividing by the sum also reduces the effect of overall brightness, such as shaded slopes or scene-to-scene differences. The best-known index is <Term id="ndvi">NDVI</Term> = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red).

<WorkedExample index="NDVI" a="B8" b="B4" points={['crops', 'west-lake']} />

Same formula, opposite signs: 0.86 for vegetation, −0.10 for West Lake water. Now do it for all 262,144 pixels:

### Example: mapping West Lake, NDWI or MNDWI?

West Lake covers roughly 5 km², yet the two water indices disagree strongly on this image:

<div class="table-wrap">

| | <Term id="ndwi">NDWI</Term> (B3, B8) | <Term id="mndwi">MNDWI</Term> (B3, B11) |
|---|---|---|
| Value at West Lake | 0.22 | 0.63 |
| Value at the Red River | 0.58 | 0.79 |
| Area ≥ 0 | 7.28 km² | 7.42 km² |
| Area ≥ 0.4 | **1.55 km²** | 6.73 km² |
| Otsu threshold | −0.04 | 0.10 |

</div>

At a threshold of 0 the two indices nearly agree. A "textbook" threshold of 0.4, however, makes NDWI miss most of West Lake, because the lake's NIR is not as low as the river's. MNDWI uses SWIR, which water absorbs almost completely, and is more robust.

<Note kind="key">

**No threshold fits every scene.** Look at the histogram, try an automatic threshold such as Otsu, and check the result on the image.

</Note>

Likewise, <Term id="ndbi">NDBI</Term> ≥ 0 covers 42 % of the frame and includes both roofs (0.21) and bare soil (0.12). NDBI alone cannot separate built-up land from bare soil.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Limitations and improved indices">

- **NDVI saturation**: as ρRed → 0, NDVI → 1 whatever the NIR, so very dense canopies become hard to tell apart (<Term id="saturation">saturation</Term>). EVI (Huete et al., 2002) reduces this:

<Formula label="EVI" tex="\mathrm{EVI} = \frac{2.5\,(\mathrm{NIR} - \mathrm{R})}{\mathrm{NIR} + 6\mathrm{R} - 7.5\mathrm{B} + 1}" where="
\mathrm{NIR},\ \mathrm{R},\ \mathrm{B} :: near-infrared, red and blue reflectance
" />

- **Soil background**: where vegetation is sparse, soil makes NDVI vary. SAVI (Huete, 1988) adds a factor L (often 0.5):

<Formula label="SAVI" tex="\mathrm{SAVI} = \frac{(1 + L)(\mathrm{NIR} - \mathrm{R})}{\mathrm{NIR} + \mathrm{R} + L}" where="
L :: soil-adjustment factor, often 0.5
" />

- **Two NDWIs**: McFeeters' (1996) NDWI uses green and NIR and maps open water. Gao's (1996) NDWI uses NIR and SWIR and measures **leaf moisture**. Always state the formula when you cite it.
- **Otsu (1979)** picks the threshold that maximises between-class variance. It works well with a clearly bimodal histogram (like MNDWI here) and poorly when one class covers very few pixels.
- Indices computed on **surface reflectance (Level-2A)** differ from those computed on top-of-atmosphere reflectance (Level-1C). Do not mix the two.

</Depth>

<Depth level="ref" title="Reference: normalized-difference indices">

<div class="table-wrap">

| Index | Formula | Sentinel-2 | Landsat 8/9 | Used for | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NDVI | (NIR − Red)/(NIR + Red) | (B8 − B4)/(B8 + B4) | (B5 − B4)/(B5 + B4) | Vegetation | Rouse et al. 1974; Tucker 1979 |
| NDWI | (Green − NIR)/(Green + NIR) | (B3 − B8)/(B3 + B8) | (B3 − B5)/(B3 + B5) | Open water | McFeeters 1996 |
| MNDWI | (Green − SWIR1)/(Green + SWIR1) | (B3 − B11)/(B3 + B11) | (B3 − B6)/(B3 + B6) | Water, incl. in cities | Xu 2006 |
| NDBI | (SWIR1 − NIR)/(SWIR1 + NIR) | (B11 − B8)/(B11 + B8) | (B6 − B5)/(B6 + B5) | Built-up / bare land | Zha et al. 2003 |
| NDMI (Gao's NDWI) | (NIR − SWIR1)/(NIR + SWIR1) | (B8 − B11)/(B8 + B11) | (B5 − B6)/(B5 + B6) | Leaf moisture | Gao 1996 |

</div>

Re-select thresholds for each scene from its histogram and check them on the image. There is no universal threshold.

</Depth>

## 11.5 NDVI through time: three rice crops in An Giang

One image is one moment. Put many dates side by side and the crop's <Term id="phenology">phenology</Term> appears. Figure 11.4 shows NDVI for a 2 × 2 km box in Tri Ton district (An Giang), where ESA WorldCover maps 96 % of the area as cropland.

<Note kind="warn">

This is an **interpretation** based on the curve's shape and the usual cropping calendar of the Long Xuyen Quadrangle. It has not been checked against actual sowing records.

</Note>

Two data-quality points stand out:

- **Clouds**: only 70 of 296 acquisitions were kept. The wet season leaves a gap of nearly three months. That is why radar is needed (Chapter 16).
- **Noise**: in January 2023 NDVI jumps 0.88 → 0.67 → 0.59 → 0.91 in just 20 days. Rice cannot wilt and recover that fast. Most likely haze or thin cloud slipped through the <Term id="cloud-mask">cloud mask</Term>. These points are kept on the chart. In analysis the series is usually smoothed (for example Savitzky–Golay) or its upper envelope is used.

<Note kind="key">

An NDVI series reveals phenology: in Tri Ton, the three 2024 peaks match three rice crops. Clouds leave gaps and noise, so check how many images survive and smooth the series before reading it.

</Note>
