## 8.1 The electromagnetic spectrum

Light, infrared and radar waves are all **electromagnetic waves**; they differ only in wavelength. Wavelength λ and frequency f are linked by the speed of light c = 299,792,458 m/s:

<Formula label="Wavelength and frequency" tex="c = \lambda \cdot f" where="
c :: speed of light, 299,792,458 m/s
\lambda :: wavelength (m)
f :: frequency (Hz)
" />

Example: Sentinel-1's radar transmits at f = 5.405 GHz, so λ = c / f = **5.55 cm**. Red light at λ = 0.665 µm has a frequency of about 451 THz.

The <Term id="em-spectrum">electromagnetic spectrum</Term> is divided into regions. Remote sensing uses mainly these:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Region | Wavelength | Main source | Information |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visible (VIS) | 0.4–0.7 µm | Reflected sunlight | Colour, pigments, water turbidity |
| Near infrared (NIR) | 0.7–1.3 µm | Reflected sunlight | Leaf structure, biomass, water boundaries |
| Shortwave infrared (SWIR) | 1.3–3 µm | Reflected sunlight | Leaf and soil moisture, minerals, cloud versus snow |
| Midwave infrared (MWIR) | 3–5 µm | Reflected and emitted | Hot spots (fires) |
| Thermal infrared (TIR) | 8–14 µm | Emitted by the surface | Surface temperature, urban heat islands |
| Microwave | 1 mm – 1 m | Radar's own pulses | Roughness, moisture, structure; through clouds |

</div>

<Note kind="key">

Every spectral region is the same kind of wave; only the wavelength differs. The wavelength decides the energy source (reflected sunlight, the surface's own emission, or the radar's pulses) and the information an image carries.

</Note>

## 8.2 Black-body radiation

Everything above 0 K emits radiation. An ideal <Term id="black-body">black body</Term> emits according to **Planck's law**: the radiation at each wavelength depends only on temperature. Two key consequences:

<Formula label="Wien's law" tex="\lambda_{max} = \frac{2898\ \mu\text{m}\cdot\text{K}}{T}" where="
\lambda_{max} :: wavelength of peak emission
T :: absolute temperature (K)
" />

<Formula label="Stefan–Boltzmann law" tex="M = \sigma T^4" where="
M :: total power emitted per square metre of surface (W/m²)
\sigma :: Stefan–Boltzmann constant, 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W·m⁻²·K⁻⁴
" />

The Sun's curve is above the Earth's at every wavelength, but sunlight reaching the Earth is greatly diluted by distance. As a result, below about 4 µm sensors record mostly **reflected** sunlight, and above about 5 µm they record mostly radiation **emitted** by the surface itself.

<Note kind="key">

Below about 4 µm a satellite measures reflected sunlight; above about 5 µm it measures radiation emitted by the surface itself. That is the line between optical and thermal imagery.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Real objects are not black bodies: emissivity">

Real objects emit less than a black body at the same temperature. The ratio is the <Term id="emissivity">emissivity ε</Term>, from 0 to 1: water about 0.99, vegetation 0.96–0.99, polished metal very low. The temperature computed directly from the measured radiance, as if the object were a black body, is the **brightness temperature**; it is lower than the true temperature. To get <Term id="lst">land surface temperature (LST)</Term> you need ε and a correction for atmospheric water vapour. The Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 product in section 8.5 does both, with emissivity from the ASTER GED database.

</Depth>

## 8.3 The atmosphere: scattering, absorption and windows

On its way down to the ground and back up, light meets two processes:

- **Scattering**: gas molecules and particles redirect light. Rayleigh scattering by molecules scales with λ⁻⁴. Blue light (0.492 µm, band B2) is scattered (0.665/0.492)⁴ = **3.3 times** more than red light (0.665 µm, band B4). This is why the sky is blue and the blue band always looks the haziest.
- **Absorption**: water vapour, CO₂ and ozone absorb energy at specific wavelengths. What remains are the <Term id="atmospheric-window">atmospheric windows</Term>.

Figure 8.2 uses the **ASTM G173** reference spectra: sunlight outside the atmosphere and at the ground after crossing 1.5 standard atmospheres. The red area between the two curves is the energy absorbed or scattered away.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Sentinel-2 band | Wavelength range | Band-averaged transmittance (direct, AM1.5) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| B2 (blue) | 459–525 nm | 68.5 % | Strongest Rayleigh scattering of the 10 m bands |
| B4 (red) | 649–680 nm | 82.4 % | Good window |
| B8 (NIR) | 780–886 nm | 87.4 % | Good window |
| B9 | 935–955 nm | 37.1 % | Placed **on purpose** in a water-vapour band to estimate water vapour |
| B10 (cirrus) | 1358–1389 nm | 0.02 % | Water vapour absorbs nearly all light from the ground: the band sees only high **cirrus** clouds |
| B11 (SWIR 1) | 1563–1657 nm | 94.3 % | Very good window |
| B12 (SWIR 2) | 2093–2278 nm | 93.2 % | Very good window |

</div>

Values are the integrated direct irradiance divided by the integrated extraterrestrial irradiance over each band, from the original G173 data (0.5–5 nm steps). Figure 8.2 shows per-wavelength values, which can differ by a few percent at band edges.

Integrated over 280–4000 nm, sunlight carries 1347.9 W/m² outside the atmosphere and 1000.4 W/m² at the ground (direct plus diffuse on the standard's 37° tilted surface). <mark>About a quarter is lost on the way down.</mark>

<Note kind="key">

Satellites see the ground only through atmospheric windows. Bands placed in absorption bands, such as Sentinel-2's B9 and B10, are not for looking at the ground but for measuring the atmosphere itself: water vapour and cirrus.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="From sensor counts to reflectance">

The sensor records **digital numbers (DN)**. Calibration turns DN into **radiance** L (W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹·µm⁻¹). Dividing by the incoming solar energy gives **top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance**:

<Formula label="Top-of-atmosphere reflectance" tex="\rho_{TOA} = \frac{\pi \cdot L \cdot d^2}{E_{SUN} \cdot \cos\theta_s}" where="
L :: measured radiance (W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹·µm⁻¹)
d :: Earth–Sun distance (astronomical units)
E_{SUN} :: extraterrestrial solar irradiance in the band
\theta_s :: solar zenith angle
" />

Atmospheric correction removes the added scattered light and the absorbed part, giving **surface reflectance** (Sentinel-2 L2A). Chapter 12 compares the two on the Hanoi image.

</Depth>

## 8.4 Reflection, absorption and transmission at the surface

Energy reaching a surface splits into **reflected**, **absorbed** and **transmitted** parts that always add up to 100 %. The reflected fraction varies with wavelength and is each surface's "signature". Leaves absorb red light strongly for photosynthesis but reflect near infrared strongly; water absorbs almost all near infrared. Chapter 11 measures these signatures on the Hanoi Sentinel-2 image: NDVI is 0.86 on floodplain crops and −0.10 on West Lake.

The absorbed energy warms the surface and is emitted again as thermal radiation. The next section measures exactly that.

<Note kind="key">

Reflected + absorbed + transmitted = 100 %. The reflected part, varying with wavelength, is the surface's signature behind the spectral indices of Chapter 11; the absorbed part warms the surface and becomes the signal of thermal imagery.

</Note>

## 8.5 Example: Hanoi's surface temperature from Landsat 9

On 22 December 2023, Landsat 9 imaged Hanoi at **10:23** local time. Its TIRS-2 sensor measures radiance at 10.6–11.2 µm, close to the Planck peak of the Earth's surface. USGS converts that radiance into surface temperature (ST_B10).

Within the 5.12 × 5.12 km frame, surface temperature ranges from 15.7 °C (2nd percentile) to 22.4 °C (98th percentile), median 19.9 °C. West Lake is about 5 °C cooler than the surrounding streets. One explanation: water has a large heat capacity and warms slowly after a winter night, while metal roofs, concrete and dry soil heat up quickly in the morning sun. This is a physical interpretation; the handbook has no measured lake temperature to check it.

<Note kind="myth">

"A Landsat thermal image tells how hot the day is." Not quite: this is **surface** temperature (roofs, water, canopies), not the **air** temperature a weather station measures at 2 m. On a summer noon a metal roof can be tens of degrees hotter than the air.

</Note>
