## 12.1 Height from two images

Look at a mountain with your left eye, then your right: the summit jumps more than the foot. That shift is <Term id="stereo-parallax">parallax</Term>, and it is proportional to height. Satellites do the same: they image an area from two positions along the orbit, separated by a **base B**, flying at height **H**.

<Figure

</Figure>

<div class="worked">

#### Example: a 100 m building in a 0.5 m stereo pair

- B/H = 0.5 with H = 694 km gives B = 347 km.
- Ground parallax: Δp = 347,000 × 100 / (694,000 − 100) = **50.0 m**, i.e. **100 pixels** of 0.5 m.
- If image matching is off by 0.3 pixel, the height error is σh = 0.3 × 0.5 / 0.5 = **0.3 m**.
- The same measurement on 30 m imagery: σh = 0.3 × 30 / 0.5 = **18 m**. That is why global 30 m DEMs have errors of metres to tens of metres.

</div>

A <Term id="dem">DEM</Term> from optical or radar imagery is a **DSM**: it measures the top surface, including canopies and roofs. A **DTM** (bare earth) needs trees and buildings removed, usually with LiDAR.

The two free global DEMs in this handbook were both measured by **radar interferometry** (Chapter 11), not by optical stereo:

| | SRTM 1″ | Copernicus DEM GLO-30 |
|---|---|---|
| Acquired | 11 days, February 2000 | 2011–2015 (TanDEM-X) |
| Radar band | C | X |
| Pixel | 1″ (~30 m) | 1″ (~30 m) |
| Heights relative to | <Term id="geoid">Geoid</Term> EGM96 | Geoid EGM2008 |

## 12.2 The DEM around Fansipan

The 18 × 18 km frame covers the Fansipan summit and Sa Pa town, from **605 m** to **3111 m** in the Copernicus DEM. The highest pixel lies 67 m from the summit coordinates. It is lower than the commonly published 3143 m because each 30 m pixel represents the whole cell, which grinds a sharp summit down.

**Three layers derived from the DEM:**

- <Term id="hillshade">Hillshade</Term>: artificial lighting from one direction. Cartographic convention puts the sun at azimuth 315° (north-west). With the light from the south-east, most people see mountains as valleys, a familiar illusion.
- <Term id="slope">Slope</Term>: **43.5 %** of the frame is steeper than 30°, and 6.4 % steeper than 45°.
- <Term id="aspect">Aspect</Term>: the direction a slope faces. South-facing slopes get more sun, which affects vegetation and temperature.

**How far apart are the two DEMs?** Copernicus minus SRTM averages **+3.5 m** with a standard deviation of **11.8 m**, and 21.8 % of pixels differ by more than 10 m. At least four causes contribute: the measurements are more than ten years apart (forest growth, new roads); different wavelengths penetrate canopies to different depths; the geoids differ (EGM96 versus EGM2008); and SRTM voids were filled from other sources. The handbook has no field GNSS heights to say which DEM is closer to the truth here.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Slope and hillshade formulas">

For the 3 × 3 window (a b c / d e f / g h i) around a pixel of size r:

<p class="formula">∂z/∂x = [(c + 2f + i) − (a + 2d + g)] / 8r     ∂z/∂y = [(a + 2b + c) − (g + 2h + i)] / 8r</p>

<p class="formula">slope = arctan √((∂z/∂x)² + (∂z/∂y)²)     hillshade = cos Z · cos S + sin Z · sin S · cos(A − aspect)</p>

Z is the sun's zenith angle (90° − elevation), A the sun azimuth, S the slope. Negative values (slopes facing away from the sun) are clipped to 0.

</Depth>

## 12.4 Terrain and radar images

Chapter 11 showed that slopes steeper than the incidence angle θ fall into **layover**, and back slopes steeper than 90° − θ into **radar shadow**. With a DEM we can compute this for every pixel. Only the slope component along the radar's look direction matters:

<p class="formula">tan(α_r) = tan(slope) · cos(aspect − direction towards the radar)</p>

Choose the **Radar distortion** layer in Figure 12.2. For an ascending Sentinel-1 pass at Fansipan's latitude the radar looks towards azimuth ≈ 78° (east-north-east); for a descending pass ≈ 282°.

| Pass, incidence | Layover | Radar shadow | Foreshortening |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ascending, θ = 30° | 9.5 % | 0.0 % | 39.1 % |
| Ascending, θ = 37° | 4.4 % | 0.1 % | 44.2 % |
| Ascending, θ = 45° | 1.5 % | 0.7 % | 47.1 % |
| Descending, θ = 37° | 2.7 % | 0.4 % | 49.2 % |

Larger incidence angles reduce layover but increase shadow. The two passes see the slopes from opposite sides, so combining ascending and descending images shrinks the area without information. That is why flood mapping in mountains (Chapter 11 exercise) needs both a DEM and both passes.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why is radar shadow rare here?">

Radar shadow needs a back slope steeper than 90° − θ, i.e. more than 53° at θ = 37°, measured along the look direction. Such slopes are rare in a 30 m DEM because each pixel already averages the cliffs. A finer DEM (1–5 m from stereo imagery) would show more shadow. Terrain analysis results depend on resolution (Chapter 4).

</Depth>
