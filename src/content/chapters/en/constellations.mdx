## 6.1 Why satellite Internet moved to LEO

Internet via <Term id="geostationary-orbit">geostationary</Term> (GEO) satellites has existed for a long time. VINASAT-1 and VINASAT-2 serve all of Viet Nam from 35,786 km. But GEO has two hard limits.

- **Latency.** A packet goes up to the satellite, down to a ground station, and the reply takes the same path back: four times 35,786 km. Video calls, games and financial trading all feel "laggy".
- **Capacity per km².** A GEO satellite sees almost half the Earth, so its bandwidth is shared by a whole continent.

In <Term id="leo">low Earth orbit (LEO)</Term> the satellites are about 65 times closer (35,786 / 550 km). Each satellite sees an area only a few hundred kilometres in radius, so the same band can be **reused** in thousands of places at once. The price: each satellite is overhead for only a few minutes (Chapter 2), so continuous coverage needs a great many satellites and the network must keep handing users over to the next one. Such a constellation is called a <Term id="megaconstellation">megaconstellation</Term>.

<Note kind="myth">

**Misconception:** "LEO Internet is faster because high-frequency signals travel faster." **In fact:** radio waves of every frequency travel at the speed of light. LEO beats GEO only because the **path is shorter**. Against fibre, LEO wins only on long routes, where light in vacuum (1.47 times faster than in glass) makes up for the trip up to the satellite and back down.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Not a new idea">

Iridium (66 satellites at about 780 km in near-polar orbits) has had inter-satellite links since the late 1990s, and Teledesic once planned hundreds of broadband satellites. Both ran into launch and manufacturing costs. Starlink differs because SpaceX launches on its own reusable rockets (Chapter 3) and builds satellites on a production line: 122 launches carried Starlink satellites in 2025 (section 6.7).

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">A LEO constellation trades the number of satellites for low latency and frequency reuse.</mark> A subscriber never talks to one satellite for long: each satellite is in the sky for only a few minutes.

</Note>

## 6.2 System architecture

A LEO Internet system has five blocks, linking back to Chapters 4 and 5:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Component | Role | Technical detail (Starlink) |
|---|---|---|
| User terminal | Talks to the serving satellite | Ku-band <Term id="phased-array-antenna">phased array</Term>, beam steered electronically, no moving parts |
| Satellite | Relays and routes | Ku phased arrays for users, Ka (and E-band from V2 Mini) for gateways, <Term id="inter-satellite-link">inter-satellite lasers</Term>, Hall thrusters |
| <Term id="gateway-station">Gateway</Term> | Connects satellites to the terrestrial network | Several tracking dishes, sited near fibre |
| <Term id="constellations-pop">PoP</Term> | Exit to the Internet | In a data centre; decides the user's IP address and apparent location |
| Global scheduler | Pairs users with satellites and beams | Measurement studies see the network reallocate about every 15 seconds (section 6.5) |

</div>

According to SpaceX's FCC-licensed filings, user links use Ku band (down 10.7–12.7 GHz, up 14.0–14.5 GHz) and gateway links use Ka band (down about 17.8–19.3 GHz, up about 27.5–30 GHz). From V2 Mini on, SpaceX added E-band (71–76 and 81–86 GHz) on gateway links for more capacity.

A packet can travel two ways. If the user and a gateway both see the same satellite, the satellite simply "bends" the signal from uplink to downlink: a <Term id="constellations-bent-pipe">bent pipe</Term>. If the user is far from every gateway (mid-ocean, in an aircraft, near the poles), the satellite passes the packet to other satellites by laser until one of them sees a gateway.

## 6.4 Designing a constellation: Walker and the number of satellites

Almost every large constellation follows a template proposed by J. G. Walker: T satellites on P circular orbital planes with the same altitude and inclination i, planes evenly spaced in right ascension of the ascending node, T/P satellites evenly spaced in each plane, and a phase offset of F·360°/T between neighbouring planes. The <Term id="walker-constellation">Walker constellation</Term> notation is **i: T/P/F**. Galileo is 56°: 24/3/1 (plus spares); a Starlink-like 53° shell, 1584/72/1, has 22 satellites per plane.

Two forms are common: **delta**, with planes spread over the full 360° (moderate inclination: GPS, Galileo, Starlink), and **star**, with near-polar planes spread over 180° (Iridium), so that ascending and descending passes do not overlap.

Three lessons from Figure 6.5 at Hanoi:

1. **36 satellites at 550 km** (6 planes × 6) cover only **17.6 %** of the time, with gaps of up to 65 minutes: a low satellite sees a very small area.
2. **The Iridium-like pattern** (66 satellites at 780 km, 86.4°, star) covers **100 %**, with 1.4 satellites in view on average: just enough to always have one.
3. **A Starlink-like shell** (1584 satellites at 550 km, 53°) also covers 100 %, but with **7.7** in view on average: many times more than coverage needs, because the goal is **capacity**.

At 480 km with ε = 25°: λ = 7.6°, a footprint radius of about **841 km**. At 1200 km (OneWeb) with the same ε: λ = 15.3°, radius 1702 km. A geostationary satellite with ε = 5°: λ = 76.3°.

This simple estimate gives exactly **66 = 11 × 6** for Iridium (780 km, 8.2° mask), the real system's operational count. At 550 km with a 25° mask about 360 are needed; at 480 km about 448.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Streets of coverage for polar orbits">

With S satellites evenly spaced in a plane, neighbouring footprints overlap when π/S < λ, and together they form a street of half-width c:

<Formula label="Street half-width and the full-coverage condition" tex="c = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\cos\lambda}{\cos(\pi/S)}\right), \qquad (P-1)(c+\lambda) + 2c \ge \pi" where="
c :: street half-width (central angle)
S :: satellites per plane
P :: number of planes
" />

Co-rotating planes can be c + λ apart if phased by half a slot; only the two counter-rotating planes at the seam must stay 2c apart. A larger S widens the street but costs satellites per plane; the best solution is the S that minimises S·P.

</Depth>

<Note kind="why" label="Link to remote sensing">

Constellations are also how remote sensing shortens revisit time. The two Sentinel-2 satellites fly the same orbit 180° apart, bringing the revisit at the equator from 10 days to 5 (Figure 2.9). Planet runs hundreds of small PlanetScope satellites to image almost all land every day, and small SAR constellations image at night and through cloud (Chapter 16). It is the same Walker problem: more satellites, denser imagery in time.

</Note>

## 6.5 User terminal and handover

The Starlink terminal is a Ku-band phased array (Chapter 5) with many small radiating elements whose phases are set so that the whole array forms one narrow beam. It does not turn mechanically to track a satellite; it **steers its beam electronically**. Each time the global scheduler reallocates, the terminal may be moved to another satellite: a <Term id="constellations-handover">handover</Term>. Independent measurement studies (Tanveer et al. 2023; Mohan et al. 2024) see latency step changes at the same seconds of every minute, **15 seconds** apart, and explain them by a scheduler that reallocates user–satellite–gateway paths on that cycle. SpaceX has not published the algorithm.

- **Field of view and obstructions.** The terminal needs a wide, clear patch of sky. The Starlink app draws an "obstruction map" from past dropouts. Trees or tall buildings blocking part of the sky cause short dropouts whenever the assigned satellite is behind them.
- **Doppler and timing.** A satellite at 480 km flies at about 7.6 km/s. At 11.7 GHz the Doppler shift reaches about 250 kHz at 25° elevation. Terminal and satellite know each other's orbits, so they pre-compensate Doppler and the changing delay.
- **Capacity is shared per cell.** Each beam serves one cell on the ground. In a crowded cell each user gets less at peak hours.
- **Rain.** In Ku band, very heavy rain slows the link or causes short dropouts (Chapter 5).

## 6.6 Inter-satellite lasers: a mesh of light in orbit

In January 2024, SpaceX engineer Travis Brashears said the Starlink network had more than **9,000 lasers**, each link sustaining 100 Gbit/s, able to connect satellites more than 5,400 km apart, and carrying more than **42 PB per day** for customers with link uptime above 99 %. 42 PB per day is an average of 3.89 Tbit/s. Lasers make it possible to:

- serve ships, aircraft, polar regions and remote islands with no gateway in a shared view;
- carry traffic to a gateway or PoP elsewhere;
- cut latency on long routes: light in vacuum is about 1.47 times faster than in optical fibre, and a laser path can stay closer to the great circle than a real cable route.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: Hanoi – Frankfurt

1. Great-circle distance: **8730 km**.
2. Fibre with route factor 1.5: 8730 × 1.5 × 1.468 / 299,792 = 64.1 ms one way, **128.3 ms** round trip.
3. LEO lower bound at 480 km (straight up, along the great circle at shell height, straight down): (2 × 480 + 8730 × 6858 / 6378) / 299,792 = 34.5 ms, **69.0 ms** round trip.
4. Illustrative laser mesh on the real snapshot (Figure 6.8): 38 satellites, path 10,976 km, **73.2 ms** round trip, only 4.2 ms above the lower bound.
5. Bent pipe down to the Phu Tho gateway, then fibre ×1.5: **135.9 ms** round trip.
6. GEO, one hop: **526 ms** round trip, more than 7 times the laser path.

This is light time. Real networks add processing, queuing and switching, so measured latency is always higher.

</div>

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">Lasers beat fibre on long routes because light in vacuum is 1.47 times faster than in glass; on short routes the trip up to the satellite and down erases that advantage.</mark>

</Note>

In 2025, 3,169 Starlink satellites were launched and 671 re-entered. Re-entries grew because the early batches reached end of life and were deorbited, while high solar activity thickened the upper atmosphere (Chapter 4).

<Note kind="myth">

**Misconception:** "More satellites means every user gets faster." **In fact:** capacity is limited **per cell** on the ground and by the licensed spectrum. A crowded cell is still slow at peak hours with dozens of satellites overhead; that is why Starlink sometimes pauses new sign-ups in busy areas.

</Note>

## 6.8 Direct-to-Cell: a base station in the sky

<Term id="constellations-direct-to-cell">Direct-to-Cell</Term> lets an ordinary LTE phone, with no special hardware, connect straight to a satellite. The satellite carries an LTE base station (eNodeB) and a large phased array and uses the mobile spectrum of a partner operator (first T-Mobile in the US). SpaceX launched the first Direct-to-Cell satellites in early 2024 and has called the service Starlink Mobile since March 2026. In September 2025 SpaceX bought EchoStar's AWS-4 and H-block spectrum (about US$17 billion) for its next Direct-to-Cell generation. On the standards side, 3GPP added non-terrestrial networks (NTN) in Release 17 (2022).

The hardest part is the **uplink budget**: a phone has an antenna of about 0 dBi and transmits about 0.2 W, far below a Starlink terminal. The shortfall must be made up by the gain of the satellite antenna, that is, its **area**. The direct competitor AST SpaceMobile goes to the extreme: each BlueBird Block 1 satellite unfolds a 64 m² array, and the first BlueBird Block 2 (Dec 2025) about 223 m².

<Formula label="Uplink budget (in dB)" tex="\frac{C}{N_0} = P_t + G_t - L_{fs} - L_{other} + G_r - 10\log_{10} T_{sys} - 10\log_{10} k" where="
P_t + G_t :: phone transmit power and antenna gain (EIRP), about −7 dBW
L_{fs} :: free-space path loss, 20·log10(4πd/λ)
G_r :: satellite antenna gain, 10·log10(4πAη/λ²) for area A and efficiency η
T_{sys} :: satellite receiver system noise temperature (K)
k :: Boltzmann constant, 10·log10 k = −228.6 dBW/(K·Hz)
" />

At 520 km and 40° elevation, with the assumptions of Figure 6.10:

- a 0.25 m² antenna gives an SNR of **−12.3 dB** in a 5 MHz channel, a Shannon limit of only 0.41 Mbit/s, and a 22.1° beam covering a cell about 203 km across;
- a 64 m² antenna gives **+11.8 dB**, a Shannon limit of about 20 Mbit/s, a 1.4° beam and a cell only about 13 km across.

A large antenna both lifts the signal above the noise and splits the ground into many small cells for frequency reuse. **Doppler** is the second challenge: at 1.9 GHz a satellite at 480 km shifts the carrier by about 41 kHz at 25° elevation, more than the 15 kHz LTE subcarrier spacing, so the base station in orbit must pre-compensate Doppler for the beam centre.

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">The phone cannot change, so every shortfall in the link budget has to be made up by a giant antenna on the satellite.</mark> That is why Direct-to-Cell has moved from texting to slow data and has not replaced the Starlink dish.

</Note>

## 6.11 Open debates

On <Term id="space-debris">space debris</Term>, ESA's Space Environment Report 2025 counts about **40,000 tracked objects** in orbit, including about **11,000 active spacecraft**, and estimates more than **1.2 million fragments** of 1–10 cm, large enough to destroy a satellite.

  items={[

    { t: 'Active', d: '≈ 11,000 spacecraft' },
    { t: '1–10 cm', d: '> 1.2 million fragments (estimated)' },
    { t: 'LEO speed', d: '≈ 7.5 km/s: a 1 cm fragment hits like a bullet' },
  ]}
  caption="The orbital environment per ESA's Space Environment Report 2025 (data to the end of 2024)."
/>

Every time two objects pass close by is a <Term id="conjunction">conjunction</Term>; when the collision probability crosses a threshold, a satellite with thrusters manoeuvres away. The long-term risk is the <Term id="kessler-syndrome">Kessler syndrome</Term>: collisions create fragments that cause more collisions. Altitude is therefore a safety choice: below about 500 km, atmospheric drag brings dead satellites and debris down quickly (<Term id="orbital-decay">orbital decay</Term>, Chapter 4). SpaceX says lowering Starlink to about 480 km lets a failed satellite re-enter within a few months. OneWeb, Qianfan and Guowang fly above 1000 km where drag is almost absent, so they must actively deorbit retired satellites.

<Tiles
  items={[
    { t: 'Astronomy', d: 'Streaks on long-exposure images; LOFAR (2023) detected unintended radio emission at 110–188 MHz from Starlink satellites. The IAU set up the Centre for the Protection of the Dark and Quiet Sky (CPS).' },
    { t: 'Orbital density', d: 'Collision risk depends on tens of thousands of satellites from many operators avoiding each other.' },
    { t: 'Atmosphere', d: 'Hundreds of satellites burn up every year (671 Starlink in 2025), leaving aluminium oxide high up; the effect on the ozone layer is being studied.' },
    { t: 'Spectrum and competition', d: 'ITU Article 22 limits EPFD so LEO systems do not interfere with GEO; disputes between constellations; the dominant role of one company.' },
    { t: 'Sovereignty and security', d: 'Many countries require domestic gateways and PoPs to manage traffic, as Viet Nam does.' },
  ]}
  caption="Five groups of open questions around megaconstellations."
/>

On spectrum, LEO constellations use Ku, Ka and higher bands (Chapter 5), the same as geostationary satellites. To avoid interference, the ITU Radio Regulations (Article 22) cap the equivalent power flux density (EPFD) that a non-geostationary system may produce; a LEO satellite switches off or steers its beam away when it is close to the line from a ground station to the geostationary arc. In Viet Nam this happens under the very sky of VINASAT-1 and VINASAT-2.

<Note kind="warn">

Starlink dims its satellites with sunshades, reflective films and attitude changes, but the number of satellites keeps growing (Figure 6.9). The astronomy problem does not go away; it shifts from "a few bright satellites" to "very many faint ones".

</Note>

<Note kind="key" label="Chapter summary">

- User ↔ satellite: Ku; satellite ↔ gateway: Ka and E-band; satellite ↔ satellite: 100 Gbit/s lasers.
- LEO gives a propagation delay of a few ms (about 25 ms measured) but needs thousands of satellites and constant handovers (about every 15 seconds).
- Capacity is limited per cell; Direct-to-Cell relies on huge satellite antennas to make up for the phone.
- In Viet Nam: a pilot until before 1 Jan 2031, up to 600,000 subscribers, 4 gateways and 1 PoP in the country, orders open since 13 Aug 2026.

</Note>
