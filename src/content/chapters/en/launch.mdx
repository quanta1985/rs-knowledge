## 3.1 Reaching orbit is about speed, not height

A sounding rocket can climb to 100 km and fall straight back a few minutes later. Height is not enough. To stay in orbit an object must **move sideways fast enough** that, while it falls, the ground curves away beneath it. Chapter 2 called this "always falling but never landing".

<Predict client:visible lang="en"
  title="How fast sideways at 400 km?"
  q="The International Space Station flies at about 400 km. How fast must it move horizontally so it does not fall to the ground?"
  slider={{ min: 0, max: 15, step: 0.1, unit: 'km/s' }} answer={7.7} tol={0.3}
  why="Circular speed at 400 km is √(μ/r) = 7.67 km/s, about 27,600 km/h — once around the Earth in just over an hour and a half. Go 41 % faster (10.84 km/s) and the object leaves the Earth for good." />

Isaac Newton imagined a cannon on top of a very tall mountain. Fire slowly and the ball lands nearby. Fire faster and it lands further away. At some speed it falls all the way around the Earth and comes back. Figure 3.1 solves that problem exactly, by numerical integration.

At 400 km, a ball fired at 6 km/s travels about 2860 km along the ground, at 7 km/s about 5190 km. At **7.67 km/s** it circles the Earth. Between 7.67 and 10.84 km/s the orbit is an ellipse with an ever higher far point. From **10.84 km/s** (escape speed, √2 times circular speed) it never comes back.

<Note kind="key">

Reaching low orbit means reaching about **7.7–7.8 km/s horizontally**. Most of a rocket's work goes into horizontal speed, not height: climbing straight up to 200 km would take only about 2 km/s, but circular speed there is 7.78 km/s.

</Note>

<Note kind="myth">

"There is no gravity in orbit, so satellites float." Wrong: at 400 km gravity is still close to 90 % of its surface value. The satellite and everything in it are in free fall together; they miss the ground only because they move sideways at 7.67 km/s.

</Note>

## 3.2 The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

A rocket speeds up by throwing gas backwards very fast. In 1903 Konstantin Tsiolkovsky wrote down how the velocity gained depends on the propellant burned — the <Term id="rocket-equation">rocket equation</Term>:

<Formula label="Tsiolkovsky rocket equation" tex="\Delta v = I_{sp}\, g_0 \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}, \qquad g_0 = 9.80665\ \text{m/s}^2" where="
\Delta v :: speed change the rocket can produce (m/s)
I_{sp} :: specific impulse (s): how good the propellant is
m_0,\ m_f :: mass at ignition and at burnout
" />

m₀ is the mass when the burn starts, m_f the mass when the propellant is gone, and <Term id="specific-impulse">Isp</Term> (in seconds) measures propellant efficiency: how many seconds one kilogram of propellant can produce a thrust equal to its own weight. Isp times g₀ is the **effective exhaust velocity** vₑ. <Term id="delta-v">Δv</Term> is the currency of every spaceflight.

The scary part is the **ln**. At Isp 300 s, a mass ratio of 10 (90 % propellant) gives 6.77 km/s. <mark>Doubling the Δv means squaring the mass ratio, to 100.</mark> This is called the "tyranny of the rocket equation".

<div class="table-wrap">

| Propellant / engine type | Examples | Typical Isp | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid | boosters, Vega, Epsilon | 250–300 s | Simple, high thrust; once lit it cannot be throttled off or restarted |
| Kerosene (RP-1) + liquid oxygen | Merlin (Falcon 9) | 300–350 s | Dense, cheap, easy to store; leaves soot in the engine |
| Methane + liquid oxygen | Raptor (Starship) | about 330–380 s | Burns cleaner, suits reuse |
| Liquid hydrogen + liquid oxygen | RS-25, Vulcain | about 450 s (vacuum) | Best chemical performance; tanks large and very cold (−253 °C) |
| Monopropellant hydrazine | small satellite thrusters | about 220–230 s | Simple and reliable, but toxic |
| Electric (Hall, ion) | Starlink, all-electric GEO satellites | 1500–3000 s and more | Very propellant-efficient but thrust only millinewtons |

</div>

The Isp values are typical ranges from Sutton & Biblarz (2017); sea-level Isp is lower than in vacuum because air pressure pushes back on the exhaust.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: what share of a Vega rocket was VNREDSat-1?

1. A Vega rocket weighs about **137 t** at lift-off (ESA). It has three solid stages, P80, Zefiro-23 and Zefiro-9, plus the liquid-fuelled AVUM upper stage.
2. Flight VV02 on 7 May 2013 carried three satellites: Proba-V about 140 kg, VNREDSat-1 about 120 kg and ESTCube-1 about 1 kg — about **261 kg** in all.
3. Payload share: 261 / 137,000 ≈ **0.19 %**. Even Vega's rated capacity (about 1500 kg to a 700 km circular orbit at 90°) is only 1500 / 137,000 ≈ **1.1 %**.

So close to 99 % of a rocket is propellant and structure; only about one per cent reaches orbit.

</div>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Deriving the rocket equation">

In a time dt a rocket of mass m throws out a mass dm of gas at speed vₑ relative to itself. Conservation of momentum (no external force) gives m·dv = −vₑ·dm. Integrating from m₀ to m_f:

<Formula tex="\Delta v = \int dv = -v_e \int_{m_0}^{m_f} \frac{dm}{m} = v_e \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}, \qquad v_e = I_{sp}\, g_0" />

Gravity and drag are external forces and so are not in the formula; they are booked separately as "losses" (section 3.4). Multiplying by g₀ is a historical convention that gives Isp the same value in metric and imperial units.

</Depth>

## 3.3 Staging and a typical flight

The way out of the ln is <Term id="staging">staging</Term>: stack several rockets, and when the lower one is empty throw away its tanks and engines. The next stage no longer accelerates dead mass, and the stages' Δv add up. That is why Vega has four stages and Ariane 5 two main stages plus two solid boosters.

Stages can be stacked two ways. **In series**: stage 2 sits on stage 1 and ignites only after stage 1 has separated (Falcon 9, Vega). **In parallel**: boosters burn alongside the core stage and drop away part-way (Ariane 5, Falcon Heavy). Upper stages usually carry a very large nozzle optimised for vacuum, so their Isp is higher than the same engine type on stage 1.

With Isp 300 s and structure at 10 % of each stage, a single stage **cannot** reach the 9.02 km/s needed even with no payload. Two identical stages lift at most about **1.7 %** of the lift-off mass to LEO, three about **2.4 %**. More stages bring diminishing returns, and every separation is another thing that can fail.

<Note kind="why" label="Why is there no single-stage-to-orbit rocket?">

Real structural ratios are about 5–10 %, while a single stage at Isp 300 s needs over 95 % propellant. Even reusable rockets such as Falcon 9 have two stages; the first stage flies back to land, which means keeping propellant in reserve and carrying less payload.

</Note>

### The ascent path: a gravity turn

A rocket does not fly "straight up, then turn sideways". It rises vertically for a few seconds to clear the tower, pitches a few degrees towards its launch azimuth, then keeps its thrust along its velocity and lets gravity gradually bend the path towards horizontal. This is a <Term id="launch-gravity-turn">gravity turn</Term>. The angle of attack stays near zero, so the long, slender vehicle avoids large side loads while the air is still dense.

About a minute after lift-off the air is still fairly dense but the rocket is already fast, so the dynamic pressure on the vehicle peaks: this is <Term id="launch-max-q">Max-Q</Term>.

<Formula label="Dynamic pressure" tex="q = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\, v^2" where="
q :: dynamic pressure (Pa), a measure of aerodynamic load on the vehicle
\rho :: air density, falling by about a factor e every 8 km of height
v :: speed relative to the air
" />

In the simulation at the top of the chapter, Max-Q comes at **T+71 s**, at **14 km**, with q ≈ **38 kPa**; stage 1 cuts off (MECO) at T+148 s at 72 km. SpaceX's real timeline below has Max-Q at T+1:10 and MECO at T+2:26: the simple model gets the order and the timescale right.

The Transporter-18 timeline (1 October 2026, flying south from Vandenberg to SSO) is similar — Max-Q T+1:04, MECO T+2:14, SECO-1 T+8:24 — but stage 1 starts a boostback burn right after separation (T+2:31) to fly back to a landing zone near the pad (T+7:32). Every such return costs propellant, so the maximum payload drops (section 3.8).

<Depth level="advanced" title="Starship: both stages designed to come back">

SpaceX's Starship has a first stage, Super Heavy, with 33 methane–oxygen Raptor engines, and a second stage, Ship, with 6 Raptors (3 optimised for sea level, 3 for vacuum). Unlike Falcon 9, both stages are designed to return and fly again. If that works, the hardware cost per flight is mostly propellant and refurbishment.

</Depth>

## 3.4 The Δv budget

The Δv a rocket must deliver is more than orbital speed. In the first minutes it climbs almost vertically, and part of its thrust only holds it up against gravity (<Term id="gravity-loss">gravity loss</Term>); air drag and steering cost a little more. On the other hand, the rocket sits on a spinning Earth and already moves east.

<Formula label="Δv budget to orbit" tex="\Delta v_{\text{engines}} = v_{\text{orbit}} - v_{\text{rot}} + \Delta v_{g} + \Delta v_{D} + \Delta v_{s}" where="
v_{\text{orbit}} :: orbital speed to reach (7.78 km/s at 200 km)
v_{\text{rot}} :: ground speed from Earth's rotation, along the launch azimuth
\Delta v_{g} :: gravity loss, the time integral of g sin γ (γ is the flight-path angle)
\Delta v_{D} :: drag loss, the time integral of D/m
\Delta v_{s} :: steering loss, when thrust does not point along the velocity
" />

The simulation at the top of the chapter tallies each term during the flight. With 15 t of payload, launched due east from 28.5° latitude:

<Stats items={[
  { v: '8.92', u: 'km/s', l: 'Δv delivered by the engines (model)' },
  { v: '0.92', u: 'km/s', l: 'gravity loss' },
  { v: '0.61', u: 'km/s', l: 'steering loss' },
  { v: '0.41', u: 'km/s', l: 'help from Earth’s rotation' },

Adding up: 7.78 − 0.41 + 0.92 + 0.02 + 0.61 ≈ 8.9 km/s, a little below the 9.3–9.5 km/s quoted in textbooks. The model also gives a maximum payload of about **25.9 t** with an expended first stage, more than the 22.8 t SpaceX publishes for Falcon 9 to LEO: a real rocket throttles around Max-Q, keeps reserve propellant and holds safety margins, all of which the model ignores.

Most communication satellites are not taken all the way to geostationary orbit by the rocket. It drops them in a <Term id="gto">geostationary transfer orbit (GTO)</Term>, an ellipse with a perigee of a few hundred km and an apogee at 35,786 km. The satellite fires its own engine at apogee to circularise and to remove the remaining inclination (section 3.7). VINASAT-1 and VINASAT-2 both took this route.

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">A rocket must deliver about 9–9.5 km/s to reach 7.8 km/s in low orbit.</mark> The 1.5–2 km/s difference is gravity and steering losses, minus the gift from Earth's rotation. The stronger the rocket (the higher its acceleration), the less time it spends "standing against gravity", and the smaller its gravity loss.

</Note>

## 3.5 Launch sites, latitude and azimuth

The ground at the equator moves east at 465 m/s. At latitude φ this falls with cos φ: 463 m/s at Kourou (5.2° N), 408 m/s at Cape Canaveral (28.6° N), 323 m/s at Baikonur (46° N). Launching east collects that gift.

More important: the orbit plane always passes through the Earth's centre and the launch site, so **an orbit's inclination cannot be lower than the launch site's latitude**. From Baikonur no rocket can fly directly into an orbit inclined less than 46°.

<Formula label="Inclination and launch azimuth" tex="\cos i = \cos\varphi\,\sin\beta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sin\beta = \frac{\cos i}{\cos\varphi}" where="
i :: orbit inclination
\varphi :: launch-site latitude
\beta :: launch azimuth, measured clockwise from north
" />

Launching due east (β = 90°) gives i = φ, the lowest possible. Launching north or south gives a polar orbit. Beyond physics, the <Term id="launch-azimuth">launch azimuth</Term> is limited by **range safety**: spent stages and debris from a failure must not fall on populated areas. That is why US polar and SSO satellites usually launch from Vandenberg (California) flying south over the Pacific, while Cape Canaveral launches only out to sea, within an eastward corridor.

<Note kind="key">

Sites near the equator matter mostly for **geostationary satellites** — not because of 140 m/s of extra rotation, but because their GTO already lies close to the equatorial plane. For Sun-synchronous remote-sensing satellites (about 98°) the launch latitude barely matters: Δv from every site in the figure differs by only a few m/s.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Retrograde launches and the cost of Earth's rotation">

When i = φ, sin β = 1: the rocket flies due east and the orbit reaches its highest latitude right over the pad. When i &lt; φ, |sin β| &gt; 1: no solution. For retrograde orbits i &gt; 90° (Sun-synchronous), sin β &lt; 0: the rocket heads slightly west of north — about 8–12° for the sites in the figure — and pays about 70–80 m/s extra.

The velocity the rocket must create is the difference between orbital and ground velocity: |v⃗ − v⃗ₑ| = √(v² + vₑ² − 2 v vₑ sin β).

</Depth>

## 3.6 Launch windows

A <Term id="launch-window">launch window</Term> is the time span in which a rocket can lift off and still reach its target orbit. The reason is geometry: an orbital plane is nearly fixed in space while the launch site turns with the Earth. Each day the site passes through a given plane only **twice**: once where the orbit's ground track heads north, once where it heads south. Lift off a few minutes early or late and you fly into a shifted plane, and correcting a plane is expensive in Δv (section 3.7).

From Cape Canaveral, the ISS plane passes over the site heading **45°** (north-east) and **135°** (south-east), **8.56 hours** apart. Only the north-east chance is used, because the south-east path runs along the US coast. The next day that chance comes about **23 minutes earlier**: partly because the Earth turns once relative to the stars nearly 4 minutes faster than a solar day, mostly because the ISS plane drifts west by about 5° a day as a result of Earth's oblateness (Chapter 2).

On top of geometry come **weather** constraints (upper-level wind shear, lightning risk, charged clouds, sea state in the booster recovery area) and **collision avoidance** (COLA): lift-off times at which the rocket or upper stage would pass too close to an object already in orbit are cut out of the window.

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">Launch windows exist because the launch site turns with the Earth through an orbital plane that is nearly fixed in space.</mark> The more a mission needs the exact plane (and the exact position in it), the shorter its window.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Timing and azimuth of the two daily chances">

Take the orbit's ascending node as the origin of (inertial) longitude. A site at latitude φ lies in the plane when its inertial longitude is Δλ past the node (track heading north) or 180° − Δλ (heading south):

<Formula tex="\sin\Delta\lambda = \frac{\tan\varphi}{\tan i}, \qquad \Delta t = \frac{180^\circ - 2\,\Delta\lambda}{360^\circ}\times 23.93\ \text{h}" where="
\Delta\lambda :: angle from the ascending node to the site, along the equator
\Delta t :: time between the northbound and the southbound chance
" />

Cape Canaveral (28.6°) to the ISS (51.6°): Δλ = 25.6°, Δt = 8.56 h. When i = φ, Δλ = 90° and Δt = 0: the two chances merge into one, due east. The J2 node drift is Ω̇ = −1.5 n J₂ (R/a)² cos i; about −5°/day for the ISS.

</Depth>

## 3.7 Hohmann transfers and plane changes

Rockets often release satellites in an intermediate orbit. The most propellant-efficient way between two circular orbits in the same plane is a <Term id="launch-hohmann">Hohmann transfer</Term>: half an ellipse tangent to both orbits, with two burns. Burn 1 in the low orbit speeds up into the ellipse; burn 2 at apogee speeds up again to circularise in the high orbit.

<Formula label="Hohmann transfer" tex="\Delta v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2r_2}{r_1+r_2}}-1\right),\quad \Delta v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_2}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{2r_1}{r_1+r_2}}\right),\quad t = \pi\sqrt{\frac{a_t^3}{\mu}}" where="
r_1,\ r_2 :: radius of the initial and target orbits (from Earth's centre)
a_t = (r_1+r_2)/2 :: semi-major axis of the transfer ellipse
t :: time to fly half the ellipse
\mu :: Earth's gravitational parameter, 398,600.4 km³/s²
" />

From a 200 km parking orbit to geostationary (35,786 km): Δv₁ = **2.455 km/s**, Δv₂ = **1.477 km/s**, **3.93 km/s** in total, taking **5.26 hours**. At apogee the satellite moves at only **1.6 km/s**, against 10.2 km/s at perigee.

Changing inclination is different: the whole velocity vector must be turned. A <Term id="launch-plane-change">plane change</Term> alone by Δi at speed v costs:

<Formula label="Orbital plane change" tex="\Delta v_{\text{plane}} = 2v\sin\frac{\Delta i}{2}, \qquad \Delta v_2 = \sqrt{v_a^2 + v_c^2 - 2 v_a v_c \cos\Delta i}" where="
v :: speed at the time of the plane change
v_a :: speed at the transfer ellipse's apogee
v_c :: circular speed in the target orbit
\Delta i :: plane-change angle
" />

Because it scales with v, a plane change is far cheaper where the satellite is slow, and folding it into the circularisation burn (the second formula, a vector sum) is cheaper still than doing the two separately.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: to GEO from Kourou, Xichang and Cape Canaveral

A 200 km parking orbit inclined at the site latitude; burn 1 at perigee is the same each time, Δv₁ = 2.455 km/s.

1. **Kourou** (5.2°): the apogee burn circularises and removes 5.2°: Δv₂ = **1.491 km/s**, total **3.945 km/s**.
2. **Xichang** (28.2°, where China launches most of its geostationary satellites): Δv₂ = **1.830 km/s**, total **4.284 km/s**.
3. **Cape Canaveral** (28.5°): Δv₂ = **1.836 km/s**, total **4.291 km/s**, **0.35 km/s** more than Kourou.
4. If, from Canaveral, the 28.5° were turned alone in the parking orbit: an extra 3.832 km/s, total **7.764 km/s**, nearly double.
5. With a satellite engine of Isp 300 s, the apogee burn eats **39.8 %** of the satellite's mass at GTO when launched from Kourou, **46.4 %** from Canaveral. Those percentage points are station-keeping propellant — years of a communication satellite's life.

</div>

<Note kind="tip">

"All-electric" communication satellites skip the Hohmann transfer: Hall thrusters push continuously from GTO to GEO for several months. An Isp of a few thousand seconds saves tonnes of propellant, at the price of a long wait before service starts.

</Note>

## 3.8 Reuse, launch prices and rideshares

An expendable rocket throws away its engines and tanks after a few minutes of flight. Falcon 9 <Term id="launch-reusable">flies its first stage back to land</Term> on a pad or a droneship and flies it again; according to the Falcon User's Guide, by February 2025 SpaceX had re-flown first stages more than 384 times, and in August 2026 one booster (B1067) flew its 37th mission. The price is the propellant kept for the return and landing: in the simulation at the top of the chapter, keeping 10 % of stage-1 propellant cuts the maximum payload from **25.9 t** to **18.9 t**.

Vega flight VV02 is a textbook secondary-payload case. The primary payload, ESA's Proba-V, was released first at about 820 km. The AVUM upper stage then fired again to lower its orbit and release VNREDSat-1 and ESTCube-1 at about 665 km. <Term id="cubesat">CubeSats</Term> released from the ISS inherit the station's orbit (about 400 km, 51.6°), which is not Sun-synchronous, and re-enter within months.

<Note kind="myth">

"Rideshares are cheap, so every satellite should take one." In exchange you cannot choose the orbit, the equator-crossing time or the date; if the primary payload slips, so do you. For a constellation that must be placed precisely in several planes, a dedicated launcher or an OTV often makes more sense.

</Note>

<Note kind="key">

The price per kg to LEO fell about 23 times from the Shuttle to Falcon 9, mainly through <mark>first-stage recovery and selling space on fully loaded flights</mark>. Small satellites benefit through rideshares, at the cost of losing the choice of orbit and date.

</Note>

Launch is still the riskiest step of a mission. On 12 Oct 2022 JAXA's Epsilon-6 deviated from its planned attitude and was commanded to self-destruct, losing every satellite on board. Even when the rocket works, the satellite may never answer: that was NanoDragon's fate in 2021.

</Note>

</Note>

## 3.10 How Viet Nam's satellites reached orbit

The six Vietnamese satellites in Figure 3.7 took three different routes. The two VINASAT communication satellites rode Ariane 5 from Kourou to GTO, each sharing the flight with another satellite. VNREDSat-1 was a secondary payload on Vega. The three small satellites made by the Vietnam National Space Center (VNSC) flew through Japanese programmes: PicoDragon from the ISS, MicroDragon and NanoDragon on Epsilon rockets of JAXA's Innovative Satellite Technology Demonstration (ISTD) programme. The LOTUSat-1 radar satellite is planned to launch on a Japanese Epsilon rocket in Japan's fiscal year 2027 (according to Japan's ambassador, VnEconomy, 20 May 2026).

#### Example: how much Δv must VINASAT-2 supply itself?

1. Ariane 5 (flight VA206, 15 May 2012, 22:13 UTC) placed VINASAT-2 in a GTO inclined 2° (ESA).
2. At apogee the satellite fires to circularise and remove the 2°: in this chapter's model (200 km perigee) that costs about **1.48 km/s**, almost the same as for a GTO exactly on the equator (1.477 km/s).
3. Launched from Baikonur, the same GTO would be inclined at least 46° and this burn would grow to **2.28 km/s**. With a satellite engine of about Isp 300 s the mass ratio rises from exp(1480 / 2942) ≈ 1.65 to exp(2280 / 2942) ≈ 2.17: the propellant for this burn goes from 40 % to 54 % of the satellite's mass at GTO.

</div>

<Note kind="key">

Viet Nam has no launch site; every Vietnamese satellite has launched abroad, and **the route to orbit depends on the satellite's size**: multi-tonne communication satellites buy space on large rockets to GTO, ~100 kg observation satellites fly as secondary payloads, CubeSats ride through cooperation programmes.

</Note>
