## 9.1 Four questions about an image

Before choosing imagery, ask four questions:

1. **How big is a pixel?** That is <Term id="spatial-resolution">spatial resolution</Term>.
2. **Which spectral bands does it have?** That is <Term id="spectral-resolution">spectral resolution</Term>.
3. **How many value levels can each pixel take?** That is <Term id="radiometric-resolution">radiometric resolution</Term>.
4. **How often is a place revisited, and how many images are cloud-free?** That is <Term id="temporal-resolution">temporal resolution</Term>.

No satellite wins on all four. Small pixels usually come with a narrow <Term id="swath">swath</Term>, so the satellite returns to the same place less often. This chapter uses the same 5.12 × 5.12 km frame around West Lake (Chapter 11) to show each trade-off.

<Note kind="key">

The four resolutions answer four questions: how big a pixel is, which bands there are, how many levels are recorded, and how many images are cloud-free. No satellite wins on all four, so choosing imagery starts from the question you need to answer.

</Note>

The area with NDVI < 0 (mostly water) **shrinks as pixels grow**: 6.98 km² with Sentinel-2, 6.67 km² with Landsat, 3.59 km² with MODIS 250 m and only 1.68 km² with MODIS 500 m. The three products were acquired on different dates, so part of the difference may come from timing (water level, crops on the floodplain). To isolate the effect of pixel size, Figure 9.2 runs a cleaner experiment.

Same image, same moment, and the NDVI < 0 area still falls from **6.98 km² (10 m)** to **4.40 km² (320 m)** and **2.87 km² (640 m)**. The cause is <Term id="mixed-pixel">mixed pixels</Term>.

### Why mixed pixels "swallow" water

Along the shore a large pixel contains both water and vegetation. Under the <Term id="linear-mixing">linear mixing model</Term>, the pixel spectrum is the area-weighted mean of the pure spectra:

<Formula label="Two-component linear mixing" tex="\rho_{pixel} = f \cdot \rho_{w} + (1 - f) \cdot \rho_{v}" where="
f :: water fraction of the pixel area
\rho_{w} :: pure water spectrum
\rho_{v} :: pure vegetation spectrum
" />

Floodplain vegetation has NIR − Red = 0.431 − 0.033 = **+0.398**. West Lake water has only 0.031 − 0.038 = **−0.007**. NDVI < 0 requires NIR < Red, i.e.:

<Formula label="Condition for a negative NDVI in a mixed pixel" tex="\begin{gathered} -0.007 \cdot f + 0.398 \cdot (1 - f) \lt 0 \\ \Rightarrow\; f \gt \frac{0.398}{0.405} \approx 98.3\ \% \end{gathered}" />

<mark>A pixel must be **more than 98 % water** before its NDVI turns negative.</mark>

<Note kind="myth">

"A mixed pixel's NDVI is the average of its components' NDVIs." Wrong: a 50/50 pixel has NDVI ≈ 0.73, not the average of the two NDVIs (0.38). Reflectance mixes linearly, but NDVI is a ratio and **does not mix linearly**.

</Note>

  source="3×3-pixel reference spectra from the Sentinel-2B image of 12 Feb 2024 (Figure 11.1). Linear mixing assumed; multiple scattering between surfaces ignored."
>
  <MixedPixel client:visible lang="en" />
</Figure>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Pixel size, GSD and 'true' resolution">

- **GSD (Ground Sampling Distance)** is the ground distance between neighbouring pixel centres. The ability to separate objects also depends on the optics' **point-spread function (PSF/MTF)**: light from one point spills into neighbouring pixels. A 10 m image therefore usually cannot separate two objects exactly 10 m apart.
- **Resampling** (e.g. 20 m → 10 m) adds pixels but **not** detail.
- **Off-nadir viewing** enlarges pixels, mostly across the swath. At the edge of the MODIS swath, pixels are several times larger than at nadir. That is why only scenes with view zenith ≤ 30° are used here.
- **Scale**: Woodcock & Strahler (1987) showed that local image variance peaks when the pixel is about ½–¾ of the object size. Choose pixels well below the size of what you measure.
- Multi-band **spectral unmixing** can estimate the fraction f per pixel instead of a hard "water / not water" label.

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

The bigger the pixel, the smaller the measured water area, even on one image (6.98 km² at 10 m, 2.87 km² at 640 m). Mixed pixels are the cause: choose pixels well below the size of the object you measure.

</Note>

## 9.3 Spectral resolution

A normal camera has three bands: blue, green, red. Sentinel-2 adds the red edge, NIR and SWIR. Figure 9.4 shows the same reference spectra seen through 3, 6 or 10 bands.

<Figure
  title="Figure 9.4 — More bands separate more surfaces"
  try="Pick 'RGB camera' and compare 'Urban trees' with 'West Lake water': the spectral distance is almost zero. Switch to 10 bands."
  source="Simulated by keeping a subset of Sentinel-2 bands (the 6-band set uses B8A, B11, B12 in place of Landsat OLI's NIR, SWIR1, SWIR2). Reference spectra as in Figure 11.1."
>
  <SpectralSampling client:visible lang="en" />
</Figure>

In the visible, urban trees and West Lake water are both dark: their spectral distance over the 3 RGB bands is only **0.013**. Adding NIR and SWIR raises it to **0.228** (6 bands) and **0.356** (10 bands), about 27 times more. By contrast, West Lake and Red River water stay close however many bands are used (0.042–0.048). Separating two kinds of water needs narrower visible bands or <Term id="hyperspectral">hyperspectral</Term> data.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Multispectral versus hyperspectral">

- **Multispectral** (Sentinel-2, Landsat): a few to a few dozen broad, non-contiguous bands.
- **Hyperspectral** (EnMAP, PRISMA): hundreds of contiguous ~10 nm bands from 400 to 2500 nm, used for minerals, algal pigments and leaf moisture. In exchange, pixels are usually 30 m and swaths narrow (~30 km).
- Spectral resolution has three parts: **number of bands**, **band position** (do they fall where surfaces differ?) and **band width** (narrow bands catch narrow absorption features).

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

Adding NIR and SWIR separates surfaces that look alike in the visible: the spectral distance between trees and water grows from 0.013 (3 bands) to 0.356 (10 bands). Two kinds of water stay close; separating them needs narrower bands or hyperspectral data.

</Note>

## 9.4 Radiometric resolution

A sensor turns a continuous signal into integers: n bits give 2ⁿ levels (<Term id="quantization">quantisation</Term>). Sentinel-2 MSI and Landsat 8 OLI use 12 bits (4096 levels), Landsat 9 OLI-2 uses 14 bits, and the older Landsat TM only 8 bits.

<Figure
  title="Figure 9.5 — Fewer bits, lost detail in dark areas"
  try="Go from 12 bits down to 4 bits with the green band and watch the orange box in West Lake. The land stays recognisable while the lake turns into flat patches."
  source="Simulated: Sentinel-2 reflectance (0–1 range) re-quantised to n bits. The original data are stored in steps of 0.0001."
>

</Figure>

In the green band, the West Lake window keeps **244** grey levels at 12 bits, **28** at 8 bits, **9** at 6 bits and only **3** at 4 bits.

<Note kind="key">

Dark surfaces such as water occupy a narrow value range (0.04–0.06), so they lose detail first when bits are removed. That matters for water-quality work, where the signal of interest is tiny.

</Note>

## 9.5 Temporal resolution

The two Sentinel-2 satellites revisit a place about every 5 days (<Term id="revisit">revisit time</Term>), or about 6 times a month. The number of **usable** images, however, depends on clouds.

<Note kind="myth">

"A 5-day revisit means a usable image every 5 days." Not in cloudy regions: clouds, not the orbit, decide how many images are usable (Figure 9.6).

</Note>

<Stats items={[

  { v: '139', l: 'usable dates in An Giang' },

Over six years Hanoi had 433 acquisition dates but only **92 usable** ones (about 15 a year). An Giang had **139**. The two regions have different cloudy seasons:

- **Hanoi**: February–March is worst (0.5–0.7 images per month), consistent with the low cloud and drizzle of late winter–spring. December is best (2.5 per month).
- **An Giang**: September is worst (0.5 per month), in the middle of the wet season. December–January is best (about 3.2–3.3 per month).

Landsat (16 days per satellite) has it harder: over Hanoi **no Landsat scene was clear from January to April 2024**, which is why Figure 9.1 uses a December 2023 scene. When clouds are the main limit, the options are multi-date composites, combining satellites (Harmonized Landsat–Sentinel) or radar (Chapter 16).

## 9.6 Choosing a sensor: always a trade-off

<div class="table-wrap">

| Question | What you need | Suggestion |
|---|---|---|
| Area of small lakes, ponds, built-up land | Pixels much smaller than the object | Sentinel-2 10 m; commercial imagery under 3 m for objects of a few tens of metres |
| Crop monitoring over a region | Many images per month | Sentinel-2 + Landsat (HLS); MODIS for very large fields |
| A big flood across a delta, daily | Wide swath, sees through cloud | MODIS for the overview; Sentinel-1 SAR under cloud |
| Water quality, minerals | Many narrow bands, many bits | Narrow Sentinel-2 / Sentinel-3 OLCI bands; hyperspectral |
| Long-term change (1980s → now) | Long, consistent record | Landsat 5/7/8/9 (Collection 2) |

</div>

<Note kind="key">

Choosing a sensor starts from three questions: how big the object is, how often it must be observed, and in which season the study area is cloudy.

</Note>
