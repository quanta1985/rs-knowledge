## 15.1 Comparing two dates: what it takes

<Term id="change-detection">Change detection</Term> answers: where did things change, from what to what, and when? The Landsat archive starts with Landsat 5 in 1984 and has been free since 2008 (Woodcock et al. 2008), so we can look back about 40 years anywhere in Viet Nam.

Two images are comparable only if they differ **mainly** because the ground changed. Every other difference is noise:

- **Season:** newly transplanted and ripe rice give very different NDVI on unchanged land. Compare the same season, or use multi-month composites.
- **Clouds and shadows:** mask them first (Chapter 12).
- **Sensor:** Landsat 5/7 (TM/ETM+) and Landsat 8/9 (OLI) have slightly different bands. The same surface gives slightly different indices (Roy et al. 2016).

This chapter uses **median composites**: for each pixel, the median of all clear observations over about 19 months. The median removes leftover clouds and dampens seasonal swings.

<Note kind="key">

Before comparing two images, remove differences that do not come from the ground: **season**, **clouds** and **sensor**. Use the same season (or multi-month composites), mask clouds carefully, and take care when the sensor changes.

</Note>

In the SWIR1–NIR–Red composite, vegetation is green while buildings and bare ground are pink-purple. Two ways to find change:

**1. Post-classification comparison** (here a simple classification with two NDVI thresholds): pixels that were "vegetated" in 2000 (NDVI ≥ 0.40) and "sparsely vegetated" in 2024 (NDVI < 0.25). Result: **15.49 km²** went from vegetation to low-vegetation surfaces, about 3.6 times the opposite direction (4.34 km²). Most of it is probably urbanisation, but it may also be land filled for future construction or fallow fields in the dry season. This is an interpretation, not checked in the field.

**2. Index differencing:** NDBI 2024 minus NDBI 2000, threshold 0.15. Subtracting directly flags **73.25 km²** of "built-up gain", a third of the frame, against only 5.14 km² of decrease. Why? The median NDBI difference over the **whole frame** is +0.094: almost every pixel "increased". That is mostly the difference between the two sensors and periods, not real construction. Subtracting the median (a simple relative normalisation) leaves 27.34 km² up and 23.37 km² down. NDVI differences are much less biased: their median is only −0.014.

<Note kind="key">

Two images from different sensors can be offset over the **whole frame**. Before thresholding an index difference, look at the median difference of the whole frame: if it is clearly not zero, most of the "change" comes from the sensors, not the ground.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why index differences break across sensors">

The OLI NIR band (845–885 nm) is narrower than the TM/ETM+ NIR band (about 770–900 nm), and SWIR1 shifts slightly too. Roy et al. (2016) derived ETM+–OLI transformation coefficients per band and for NDVI. Here the handbook only uses relative normalisation (subtracting the frame median). That assumes most of the frame did **not** change, which is not true for a fast-urbanising area like west Hanoi, so the normalised result is illustrative only. Better: apply cross-sensor coefficients, or use "invariant" pixels (deep water, old roofs) as anchors.

</Depth>

## 15.3 Following each pixel for 25 years

<mark class="key">Two dates only tell you **that** something changed. To know **when**, you need a <Term id="time-series">time series</Term>.</mark> Figure 15.2 takes the median NDVI of **each year** 2000–2024 in the central part of the frame (7.5 × 7.5 km) and looks for the year NDVI dropped abruptly.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: pixel row 202, column 206

NDVI in 2000–2007: 0.65, 0.62, 0.60, 0.59, 0.59, 0.72, 0.66, 0.59. From 2008 to 2024 it stays at 0.13–0.23, except 2012 (0.31).

1. Try every split. Splitting at **2008** gives the smallest sum of squared errors.
2. Level before: mean of 2000–2007 = 5.02 / 8 ≈ **0.63**.
3. Level after: mean of 2008–2024 = 3.22 / 17 ≈ **0.19**.
4. F = 197: a "single step" model fits far better than a straight line.
5. The Theil–Sen trend of the whole series is −0.18 per decade. True, but it hides the story: the change happened **within one year**, not gradually.

Interpretation (not verified): a field or garden filled for construction around 2008. 2008 is also the year Ha Tay was merged into Hanoi, but that is context, not evidence for this pixel.

</div>

In the whole 56.25 km² frame, **6008 pixels (5.41 km²)** show an NDVI drop of at least 0.15 with F ≥ 20. Two cautions when reading the break-year map:

- **2004** has the most pixels (1311). Partly because 2004 is the **earliest** year the algorithm may choose (it needs at least 4 years before the split). Partly because from May 2003 Landsat 7's scan line corrector failed (SLC-off), leaving stripes without data.
- **2012** has almost only Landsat 7 SLC-off (Landsat 5 stopped imaging at the end of 2011, Landsat 8 launched in 2013). The median number of clear observations in 2012 is just **1**, so that year's NDVI is very noisy.

<Note kind="key">

A break-year map reflects the data and the algorithm as well as the ground. Pixels piling up in the earliest allowed year (2004) or in a data-poor year (2012) need a careful look before they count as real change.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Theil–Sen, breakpoints and real algorithms">

**Theil–Sen** (Sen 1968): the slope is the median of the slopes of all point pairs. One noisy year (like 2012) barely moves it, whereas least squares is pulled off.

The **breakpoint** here is the simplest model: two constant levels. Research algorithms go further: LandTrendr (Kennedy et al. 2010) fits several line segments; BFAST (Verbesselt et al. 2010) separates trend from seasonality; CCDC (Zhu & Woodcock 2014) uses every image without annual compositing. LandTrendr and CCDC both run in Earth Engine.

</Depth>

## 15.4 Accretion and erosion at Ca Mau cape

Ca Mau cape is where Viet Nam's land is still changing fast. Figure 15.3 uses <Term id="mndwi">MNDWI</Term> (Chapter 11) to separate water from land at two dates, then compares them.

With an MNDWI threshold of 0 and the sea only, this frame shows **21.09 km² of accretion** (sea became land) and **1.01 km² of erosion** (land became sea), a net land gain of about 0.84 km² per year. Accretion concentrates on the western shore and erosion along the south-eastern shore, consistent with the general picture of the cape growing into the Gulf of Thailand and eroding on the East Sea side.

The result is very sensitive to the method:

<div class="table-wrap">

| MNDWI threshold | Counted on | Accretion (km²) | Erosion (km²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Sea only | 21.09 | 1.01 |
| 0 | All water | 31.17 | 5.36 |
| −0.1 | Sea only | 45.83 | 1.08 |
| +0.1 | Sea only | 19.09 | 1.02 |

</div>

"All water" also counts shrimp ponds dug or filled inland, which is not the coastline. A threshold of −0.1 puts wet mudflats into "water" in 2000, so when mangroves colonise them the "accretion" doubles. One more source of error is the **tide**: images at low tide expose more mudflat. Median composites reduce this but do not remove it.

<Note kind="key">

Accretion and erosion figures depend on the MNDWI threshold, on counting "sea only" or "all water", and on the tide at acquisition. In this frame, moving the threshold from 0 to −0.1 more than doubles the accretion (21.09 → 45.83 km²).

</Note>

## 15.5 Reporting change properly

<Note kind="key">

Change is usually a rare class. Report change areas from a stratified validation sample with confidence intervals, together with the rule used (index, threshold), never by counting pixels.

</Note>
<Depth level="ref" title="Checklist">

1. Same season or multi-month composites; mask clouds carefully.
2. Across sensors: cross-calibrate (Roy et al. 2016) or compare after classification.
3. State the rule (index, threshold) and test its sensitivity.
4. Never report change area by counting pixels. Draw a stratified validation sample over the change classes and estimate areas with confidence intervals (Olofsson et al. 2014, Chapter 14). Change is usually a rare class and easily mis-estimated.
5. In time series: do not join points across long gaps, flag data-poor years (such as 2012), and distrust breaks piled up at the start or end of the series.

</Depth>
