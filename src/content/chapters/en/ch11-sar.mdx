## 11.1 Why radar

In Chapter 4, only **92 of 433 Sentinel-2 acquisition dates** over West Lake in 2022–2024 were clear enough to use. Optical sensors measure reflected sunlight, so clouds mean missing data.

<Term id="sar">Synthetic aperture radar (SAR)</Term> is an **active** sensor: the satellite transmits microwave pulses and records the echoes. Sentinel-1's C-band waves are about 5.5 cm long, far longer than cloud droplets, so they pass through clouds almost unattenuated, day or night. The price: radar images have no colour and are harder to read than optical ones.

<Depth level="ref" title="Reference: common radar bands">

<div class="table-wrap">

| Band | Wavelength | Example satellites | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | ~3 cm | TerraSAR-X, COSMO-SkyMed | High detail; scatters from the top of canopies |
| C | ~5.5 cm | Sentinel-1, RADARSAT | Balanced; open data, regular global coverage |
| L | ~24 cm | ALOS-2 PALSAR-2, NISAR | Penetrates forest canopies; more stable interferometry over vegetation |
| P | ~70 cm | BIOMASS | Forest biomass |

</div>

Sentinel-1: 5.405 GHz (λ ≈ 5.55 cm); the IW mode covers a ~250 km swath, GRD products are sampled at 10 m. In 2023–2024 only Sentinel-1A was operating (Sentinel-1B failed at the end of 2021), so each orbit repeats every 12 days.

</Depth>

## 11.2 Side-looking geometry

A radar cannot look straight down: two points symmetric about the ground track would echo back at the same time and could not be separated. Sentinel-1 therefore **looks to the right** of its flight direction. The angle between the beam and the vertical at the ground is the <Term id="incidence-angle">incidence angle θ</Term>.

At θ = 37° the off-nadir angle at the satellite is only 32.9° because the Earth is curved. The beam travels **845 km** to the ground, and the target lies **459 km** from the ground track. Radar measures the echo's travel time, which is slant range; to get ground distance, divide by sin θ:

<p class="formula">ground-range resolution = slant-range resolution / sin θ = 2.65 m / sin 37° ≈ 4.4 m</p>

The smaller the incidence angle (near the track), the smaller sin θ, so the coarser the ground pixel.

### Terrain distorts the image

On sloping ground the local incidence angle depends on the slope. Three effects are common in northern and central Viet Nam's mountains:

- **Foreshortening:** a slope facing the radar, less steep than θ, looks compressed and bright.
- **Layover:** a slope steeper than θ; the summit is closer to the radar than the foot, so it appears **before** the foot in the image.
- **Shadow:** a back slope steeper than 90° − θ receives no signal and is completely dark, with no information.

The West Lake frame is almost flat, so these effects do not show here. In mountains, Sentinel-1 images need terrain correction with a DEM (Chapter 12). Earth Engine applies it to `COPERNICUS/S1_GRD` with SRTM 30 m, but it **cannot** recover information in shadow and layover areas.

## 11.3 Backscatter and polarisation

A radar pixel stores the <Term id="backscatter">backscatter coefficient σ⁰</Term>, the fraction of energy scattered back to the satellite, usually in <Term id="decibel">decibels</Term>: σ⁰(dB) = 10·log₁₀ σ⁰. Every −10 dB is ten times less energy. Four mechanisms set σ⁰:

1. **Specular reflection:** calm water reflects the wave away from the satellite, so it looks **dark**.
2. **Surface scattering:** the rougher the soil (relative to the 5.5 cm wavelength), the brighter.
3. **Volume scattering:** the wave bounces many times inside canopies and rice fields, so the target looks moderately bright and the signal is **depolarised**.
4. **Double bounce:** walls and ground form corner reflectors that send energy straight back, so the target looks **very bright**.

<Term id="polarization">Polarisation</Term> is the direction in which the wave oscillates. Sentinel-1 transmits vertically (V) and receives both vertically (VV) and horizontally (VH). VH only carries signal when the wave is depolarised, mainly by volume scattering in vegetation.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Point | VV (dB) | VH (dB) | Main mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roofs and streets | −3.0 | −5.9 | Double bounce: brightest |
| Floodplain vegetation | −9.7 | −14.1 | Volume scattering in crops |
| Urban trees | −9.4 | −15.7 | Volume scattering in canopies |
| Bare soil / sand | −15.9 | −27.9 | Surface scattering, little depolarisation (VV − VH = 12 dB) |
| West Lake water | −16.9 | −20.9 | Specular, possibly wind-roughened |
| Red River water | −20.4 | −23.0 | Specular: darkest |

The Red River and the built-up point differ by 17.4 dB, a power ratio of about 55. That is why water is the easiest target to separate in radar images. Floodplain vegetation and urban trees have almost the same VV and differ by only 1.6 dB in VH. A single radar image struggles to tell vegetation types apart; a time series does better (section 11.6).

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why is West Lake (−16.9 dB) brighter than the Red River (−20.4 dB)?">

Water is dark only when it is smooth compared with the wavelength. Wind ripples of a few centimetres on a wide lake send some energy back. In a 20 × 20 pixel window in the middle of the lake the mean VV is −18.3 dB, lower than the 3 × 3 point above because of speckle (section 11.4). The −15 dB water threshold in section 11.5 leaves room for this. This is a physical interpretation; the handbook has no wind measurements at the lake on 12 Feb 2024.

The bright cross in the upper left of the image is the **sidelobe** pattern of a very strong reflector (usually a metal structure): its energy leaks along the range and azimuth directions. It is a property of SAR image formation, not a cross-shaped object.

</Depth>

## 11.4 Speckle

Each radar pixel is the sum of echoes from many small scatterers inside it. They add up or cancel depending on their phase, so two neighbouring pixels on the same lake can differ by several dB. This salt-and-pepper texture is <Term id="speckle">speckle</Term>. It is **multiplicative**: it scales with the signal instead of being added like electronic noise.

In Figure 11.3, pick a filter and read "VV std. dev., West Lake" in the panel:

| Filter | VV standard deviation in a 20 × 20 px window in West Lake |
|---|---|
| None | 2.04 dB |
| Boxcar 5 × 5 | 0.94 dB |
| Lee 5 × 5 | 0.97 dB |
| Boxcar 9 × 9 | 0.56 dB |

Larger windows give smoother images but **blur shorelines and streets**. The Lee (1980) filter addresses this: in homogeneous areas it averages like a boxcar, but where the variance exceeds what speckle alone would cause (usually at edges) it stays close to the original value. Inside the lake both filters reduce speckle about equally; zoom in on the West Lake shore to see where they differ.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Average in power, not in dB">

dB is logarithmic. The mean of dB values is always **lower** than the dB of the mean power (Jensen's inequality). Every filter and zonal statistic in this handbook converts to linear power 10^(dB/10), computes, then converts back. The Lee filter uses the equivalent number of looks ENL = 4.4 of IW GRDH products:

<p class="formula">I' = m + W (I − m),  W = max(0, 1 − Cu² / Ci²),  Cu² = 1/ENL,  Ci² = var / m²</p>

</Depth>

## 11.5 Example: mapping floods after Typhoon Yagi

Typhoon Yagi made landfall in Quang Ninh – Hai Phong on 7 September 2024. Heavy rain afterwards raised the Red River through Hanoi and flooded many riverside areas. The sky was mostly cloudy, so optical images were of little use. Sentinel-1 has two images from the same descending orbit 91, on 31 August (before) and 12 September (after), exactly one 12-day cycle apart.

Three methods, three answers:

| Method | Water on both dates | New water after the typhoon |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold −15 dB | 6.75 km² | 0.97 km² |
| Otsu threshold on the post image (−11 dB) | 7.47 km² | 1.57 km² |
| Change detection, VV drop > 3 dB | 6.75 km² | 2.41 km² |

Change detection finds **2.5 times** the area of the −15 dB threshold, mostly on the floodplain east of the Red River. At the Chapter 6 floodplain-vegetation point, for example, VV fell from −7.9 dB to −16.3 dB. When crops are partly submerged, leaves still stick out of the water, so σ⁰ drops sharply but may not fall below the open-water threshold. Otsu picks −11 dB because the frame is mostly urban and the histogram has no clear second peak, so this automatic threshold easily counts wet soil and dark areas as "water".

**Before using the result:**

- This is a **likely-flooded** map, not validated by field data or aerial images. Do not use it as damage statistics.
- Flooded buildings often **brighten** (walls and water form double bounce), so both methods miss flooding in built-up areas.
- 12 September is a single snapshot; the flood peak may have been on another day.

## 11.6 Example: monitoring rice under clouds

The Chapter 6 NDVI series for An Giang rice fields has a long hole: **no clear Sentinel-2 image from 18 June to 16 September 2024**, the middle of the rainy season. Over the same period Sentinel-1 orbit 26 acquired 6 images.

VH drops on 15 August (−21.0 dB) and 27 August 2024 (−23.4 dB), then recovers to −17.4 dB on 8 September and −16.1 dB on 20 September. When NDVI returns it reads 0.60 (16 September) and then 0.79 (26 September). A consistent reading: in late August the fields had just been harvested and flooded for land preparation and sowing (open water, so low VH); then the new crop grew and volume scattering raised VH. This is an **interpretation not checked against a field crop calendar**, but it matches the two-to-three rice crops per year typical of An Giang.

## 11.7 Radar interferometry (InSAR) and subsidence

<Depth level="advanced" title="Measuring ground motion with the phase">

Besides intensity, radar records the **phase** of the echo. Comparing the phase of two images taken from nearly the same position at different times gives an <Term id="insar">interferogram</Term>. If the ground moves away from the radar by half a wavelength (λ/2 ≈ 2.8 cm for Sentinel-1) along the line of sight, the phase turns one full cycle and draws one fringe. Stacking tens to hundreds of images (PS-InSAR, SBAS) measures subsidence of millimetres per year.

The Mekong Delta is the textbook case. Erban et al. (2014) measured average subsidence of the order of 1–2 cm per year with InSAR, locally several centimetres. Minderhoud et al. (2017) showed with a model that groundwater extraction is the main driver. Such rates are many times faster than sea-level rise.

There is no interactive InSAR figure yet: phase (SLC) data are not available in Earth Engine. To practise, use the free ASF HyP3 service or ESA's SNAP software.

</Depth>
