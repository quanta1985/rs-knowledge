## 2.1 Gravity and Kepler's three laws

A satellite **is always falling**. It misses the ground because it moves sideways fast enough: while it falls, the curved surface drops away by the same amount. What pulls it down is Newton's gravity between the Earth and the satellite:

<Formula label="Gravity and the standard gravitational parameter" tex="F = \frac{G M_\oplus m}{r^2}, \qquad \mu \equiv G M_\oplus = 398\,600.4\ \text{km}^3/\text{s}^2" where="
r :: distance from the Earth's centre to the satellite (km)
m :: mass of the satellite
\mu :: the Earth's standard gravitational parameter, known far more precisely than G or M alone
" />

<Predict client:visible lang="en"
  title="How much gravity is left on a space station?"
  q="Astronauts on the International Space Station (about 400 km up) float. What percentage of surface gravity acts on them there?"
  options={["Almost 0 %", "About 10 %", "About 50 %", "About 90 %"]} answer={3}
  why="Gravity falls off as 1/r². At 400 km, r is only 6 % larger than the Earth's radius, so g is still (6378/6778)² ≈ 88.5 %. Astronauts float because the station and the crew fall freely together, not because gravity is gone." />

Orbital mechanics almost always uses the product μ = GM instead of G and M separately. The Earth's equatorial radius is 6378.137 km (WGS84), its mean radius 6371 km. At 400 km, gravity is still **88.5 %** of its surface value.

Johannes Kepler derived three laws from Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars (1609 and 1619). Newton later proved all three from the law of gravitation. They hold for artificial satellites around the Earth:

<Tiles items={[
  { t: 'Law 1: orbits are ellipses', d: 'The Earth sits at one focus. A circular orbit is an ellipse with zero eccentricity. The closest point is perigee, the farthest apogee.' },
  { t: 'Law 2: equal areas', d: 'The line from the Earth to the satellite sweeps equal areas in equal times: fast at perigee, slow at apogee (conservation of angular momentum).' },
  { t: 'Law 3: T² is proportional to a³', d: 'The period depends only on the semi-major axis a, not on eccentricity or the satellite’s mass.' },
]} />

<Formula label="Kepler's third law" tex="T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}" where="
T :: orbital period (s)
a :: semi-major axis (km); for a circular orbit a = r = R⊕ + h
" />

### Circular speed, escape speed and the vis-viva equation

On a circular orbit gravity provides the centripetal force: μm/r² = mv²/r. Hence:

<Formula label="Circular speed and escape speed" tex="v_{c} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}, \qquad v_{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}} = \sqrt{2}\,v_{c}" where="
v_{c} :: speed needed to fly a circle of radius r
v_{esc} :: minimum speed to leave the Earth for good from radius r
" />

<Stats items={[
  { v: '7.91', u: 'km/s', l: 'circular speed at the surface (first cosmic velocity)' },
  { v: '11.18', u: 'km/s', l: 'escape speed from the surface (second)' },
  { v: '7.67', u: 'km/s', l: 'circular speed at 400 km (ISS)' },
  { v: '3.07', u: 'km/s', l: 'circular speed in geostationary orbit' },
]} caption="Computed from μ = 398,600.4 km³/s² and the 6378.137 km equatorial radius (scripts/ch02_numbers.ts)." />

On an elliptical orbit the speed at any distance r is given by the **<Term id="ch02-vis-viva">vis-viva</Term> equation**, the most useful tool for orbit changes:

<Formula label="Vis-viva and specific energy" tex="v^2 = \mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right), \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{r} = -\frac{\mu}{2a}" where="
v :: speed at radius r (km/s)
a :: semi-major axis; a = r on a circle, a → ∞ for an escape orbit
\varepsilon :: specific energy (per kg); negative means the satellite is bound to the Earth
" />

<Note kind="myth">

"Higher orbits are faster." The opposite: a geostationary satellite moves at 3.07 km/s, the ISS at 7.67 km/s. But **reaching** a higher orbit costs energy: ε rises from −29.40 km²/s² at 400 km to −4.73 km²/s² in geostationary orbit. The extra energy goes into potential energy, not speed. Changing orbit with two burns (Hohmann) is in Chapter 3.

</Note>

<div class="worked">

#### Example: Sentinel-2 at 786 km

1. Orbit radius: r = 6378.1 + 786 = 7164.1 km.
2. Velocity: v = √(398,600.4 / 7164.1) = **7.46 km/s**.
3. Period: T = 2π √(7164.1³ / 398,600.4) = 6035 s = **100.6 min**.
4. Revolutions per day: 1440 / 100.6 ≈ **14.3**. ESA designed exactly 14 3/10 revolutions per day so the ground track repeats after 10 days (143 orbits).

#### Example: geostationary orbit and GTO

1. A geostationary satellite needs T equal to one **sidereal day**: 86,164 s, the time the Earth takes to turn 360° relative to the stars, 3 min 56 s shorter than the solar day.
2. Inverting Kepler III: a = ∛(μ (T/2π)²) = **42,164 km**, an altitude of **35,786 km** and a speed of **3.07 km/s**.
3. A GTO with a 250 km perigee and a 35,786 km apogee: a = (6628 + 42,164)/2 = 24,396 km, e = 0.728, period **10.5 hours**.
4. Vis-viva: v = **10.19 km/s** at perigee and only **1.60 km/s** at apogee. The ratio 6.36 equals r_apogee / r_perigee, as law 2 predicts.

</div>

## 2.2 The six Keplerian elements

To know where a satellite is at a given time takes exactly **6 numbers**: the equivalent of 3 position coordinates plus 3 velocity components. The easiest set to picture is the <Term id="ch02-orbital-elements">Keplerian elements</Term>, in three groups:

<Tiles items={[
  { t: 'Size and shape: a, e', d: 'The semi-major axis a sets the period and energy. The eccentricity e sets the shape: r_perigee = a(1−e), r_apogee = a(1+e).' },
  { t: 'Orientation: i, Ω, ω', d: 'Inclination i and right ascension of the ascending node Ω place the orbit plane in space; the argument of perigee ω turns the ellipse inside that plane.' },
  { t: 'Position: ν (or M)', d: 'The true anomaly ν is the angle from perigee to the satellite. The mean anomaly M carries the same information as if the satellite moved uniformly.' },
]} />

The angles are measured in the **Earth-centred inertial frame (ECI)**: the x axis points to the **vernal equinox ♈** (the Sun's direction at the March equinox), the z axis to the north pole. The orbit plane cuts the equatorial plane along the **line of nodes**. The point where the satellite crosses the equator northbound is the <Term id="ch02-raan">ascending node ☊</Term>.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Symbol | Name | Meaning | Typical values |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | Semi-major axis | Size of the orbit; sets the period and energy | LEO 6800–7200 km; GEO 42,164 km |
| e | Eccentricity | 0 is a circle, 0–1 an ellipse | Earth-observation satellites ~0.0001–0.001; GTO ~0.73; Molniya ~0.74 |
| i | Inclination | Angle between orbit plane and equator; above 90° is retrograde | ISS 51.6°; GPS 55°; SSO ~98° |
| Ω | Right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN) | Turns the plane around the polar axis, measured from ♈ to the ascending node | The planes of a constellation are evenly spaced in Ω |
| ω | Argument of perigee | Where perigee lies in the plane, from the ascending node in the direction of flight | Molniya 270°: apogee hangs over the northern hemisphere |
| ν or M | True / mean anomaly | Where the satellite is at the reference time (epoch) | Changes continuously 0–360° |

</div>

<Formula label="Radius from the true anomaly" tex="r = \frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e\cos\nu}, \qquad r_p = a(1-e), \quad r_a = a(1+e)" where="
\nu :: true anomaly: ν = 0 at perigee, 180° at apogee
r_p,\ r_a :: perigee and apogee radius (subtract 6378 km for altitude)
" />

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">a and e give size and shape, i, Ω and ω point the orbit in space, ν places the satellite on it.</mark> A circular orbit needs only a and i for its shape and tilt; ω loses its meaning when there is no perigee.

</Note>

<Note kind="why">

Inclination is limited by the latitude of the launch site: a direct launch from latitude φ reaches only i ≥ φ. Getting down to i = 0° for a geostationary satellite takes extra propellant to "bend" the plane, so sites near the equator have an edge. Chapter 3 works out the cost.

</Note>

<Note kind="warn">

TLE elements are mean elements tied to SGP4, not the "true" Keplerian elements at an instant. Feeding them straight into two-body formulas gives wrong answers. A TLE is good to about 1 km at epoch and degrades by a few km per day, worst in low orbits where drag follows solar activity (Vallado et al., 2006). Operators use far better orbits: Sentinel-1's post-processed orbits are accurate to about 5 cm, good enough for measuring ground deformation with InSAR (Chapter 16).

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="ECI, ECEF and time scales">

Keplerian elements live in the **ECI** frame (inertial, not turning with the Earth). Maps and GNSS use **ECEF** (fixed to the Earth, turning with it). The two differ by a rotation about z by **Greenwich mean sidereal time** (GMST), which grows 360.9856° per solar day; the figures in this chapter use the IAU 1982 formula. Exact conversion also needs precession, nutation and polar motion (Vallado 2013).

Time has several scales too. **TAI** is International Atomic Time, perfectly uniform. **UTC** follows the Earth's rotation with leap seconds: since 1 January 2017, TAI − UTC = 37 s. **GPS time** equals TAI − 19 s and has no leap seconds, so today GPS − UTC = 18 s (IERS Bulletin C). TLE epochs are in UTC.

</Depth>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why does Molniya use i = 63.4°?">

J2 (section 2.4) makes the argument of perigee ω drift at a rate proportional to (5cos²i − 1). At cos²i = 1/5, i.e. **i = 63.4°** or 116.6°, the drift vanishes. This is the <Term id="ch02-critical-inclination">critical inclination</Term>. Thanks to it, Molniya's apogee always stays over the northern hemisphere, where the satellite is slow: it spends about 11 of every 12 hours north of the equator. Figure 2.7 shows the perigee curve crossing zero at exactly 63.4° at every altitude.

</Depth>

## 2.4 Perturbations: why real orbits are not perfect ellipses

Kepler's laws hold only for the two-body problem with a perfectly spherical Earth. In reality several small forces change the orbit gradually:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Perturbation | Cause | Main effect | Strongest in |
|---|---|---|---|
| <Term id="j2">J2</Term> | The Earth is oblate: equatorial radius 21.4 km larger than polar; J2 = 1.08263×10⁻³ | The orbit plane turns (node drift), perigee drifts | LEO; the largest perturbation |
| Atmospheric drag | Thin air remains at a few hundred km; density rises sharply with solar activity | Energy loss, the orbit decays and re-enters | Below ~700 km |
| Moon and Sun | Third-body pull | Geostationary inclination grows by about 0.75–0.95°/year | GEO, HEO |
| Solar radiation pressure | Photons carry momentum and push on the panels | Seasonal changes in eccentricity | Light satellites with large area, high orbits |
| Equatorial asymmetry (J22) | The equator is slightly elliptical | Geostationary satellites drift in longitude toward two stable points near 75°E and 105°W | GEO |

</div>

J2 is the first (and largest) term in the expansion of the Earth's gravity field. The equatorial bulge tugs on the orbit, making the **orbit plane precess** around the Earth's axis and **perigee drift** within the plane. The time-averaged ("secular") rates are:

<Formula label="J2 drift of the node and perigee" tex="\dot\Omega = -\frac{3}{2}\, n J_2 \left(\frac{R_\oplus}{p}\right)^{2}\cos i, \qquad \dot\omega = \frac{3}{4}\, n J_2 \left(\frac{R_\oplus}{p}\right)^{2}\left(5\cos^2 i - 1\right)" where="
n = \sqrt{\mu/a^3} :: mean angular rate of the satellite (rad/s)
p = a(1-e^2) :: semi-latus rectum (equal to a on a circle)
R_\oplus :: equatorial radius 6378.137 km
\cos i :: its sign sets the direction of the node drift
" />

For the ISS (420 km, 51.6°) the node regresses **4.95° per day** westward and perigee advances 3.70°/day. At GPS altitude (20,200 km) J2 is much weaker: the node drifts only 0.039°/day.

Drag is proportional to air density, which falls roughly exponentially with altitude but, at a given altitude, can differ tens of times between a quiet and an active Sun. A real case: on 3 February 2022 SpaceX launched 49 Starlink satellites into an orbit with a perigee of about 210 km. A geomagnetic storm heated and expanded the atmosphere, raising drag by up to about 50 % over previous launches. SpaceX reported that up to 40 of the satellites would re-enter or already had (SpaceX, 8 Feb 2022). </Note>

## 2.5 Sun-synchronous orbits (SSO)

The Earth goes around the Sun by 360° in 365.24 days, **0.9856° per day** eastward. If the inclination makes dΩ/dt exactly +0.9856°/day, the orbit plane follows the Sun: the angle between the plane and the Sun's direction stays the same all year. The satellite then passes each latitude at the same local time. That is a <Term id="sun-synchronous-orbit">sun-synchronous orbit</Term>.

A positive dΩ/dt needs a negative cos i, i.e. **i above 90°**: the satellite flies slightly against the Earth's rotation. Solving the J2 formula for dΩ/dt = +0.9856°/day:

<Formula label="Sun-synchronous inclination" tex="\cos i_{SSO} = -\,\frac{\dot\Omega_{\odot}}{\tfrac{3}{2}\, n J_2 \left(R_\oplus/a\right)^2}, \qquad \dot\Omega_{\odot} = \frac{360^\circ}{365.2422\ \text{d}} = 0.9856^\circ/\text{d}" where="
\dot\Omega_{\odot} :: rate at which the Sun moves east around the sky
i_{SSO} :: 97.4° at 500 km; 98.2° at 705 km; 98.5° at 786 km
" />

  title="Figure 2.8 — The orbit plane over a year: sun-synchronous or not"

>

The J2 formula applied to real satellites (CelesTrak elements from Chapter 1):

<div class="table-wrap">

| Satellite | Altitude | Actual inclination | J2 sun-synchronous inclination | Plane rotation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sentinel-2A | 789.0 km | 98.56° | 98.56° | 0.986°/day |
| Landsat 9 | 702.5 km | 98.21° | 98.20° | 0.988°/day |
| Sentinel-1A | 693.7 km | 98.16° | 98.16° | 0.985°/day |
| VNREDSat-1 | 677.8 km | 97.89° | 98.10° | 0.961°/day |

</div>

The first three match the theory almost exactly: they are actively kept in their orbits. VNREDSat-1 is 0.21° off, so its overpass time is drifting (Chapter 1).

**Why optical remote sensing likes SSO:**

- **Repeatable lighting.** The same local time means a similar Sun angle from image to image (changing only with the seasons), which makes time series comparable.
- **A chosen imaging time.** A local time of the descending node (<Term id="ltdn">LTDN</Term>) of about 10:00–10:30 balances a high enough Sun against afternoon convective clouds that have not yet built up. Landsat 8/9 cross at about 10:00, Sentinel-2 at 10:30.
- **Dawn–dusk orbits.** Nodes at 06:00/18:00 put the orbit plane nearly perpendicular to the Sun's direction, so the solar panels are almost always lit. Ideal for power-hungry SAR such as Sentinel-1 (ascending node 18:00).

<Note kind="myth">

"Sun-synchronous satellites are always in sunlight." Only dawn–dusk orbits come close, and even they pass through the Earth's shadow during a short season around one of the solstices. A 10:30 SSO like Sentinel-2 enters the shadow on the night side every orbit, for about a third of the time (Chapter 4 computes this eclipse share).

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="The altitude limit of sun-synchronous orbits">

Higher up, J2 weakens (through (Re/a)² and n). To still turn 0.9856°/day, cos i must become more negative. Near 6000 km it needs cos i ≈ −1, i.e. i ≈ 180°; above that no inclination works, and Figures 2.1 and 2.7 show "none". In practice sun-synchronous orbits are used almost only below 1000 km.

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">SSO exploits the J2 perturbation: an inclination of about 97–99° makes the orbit plane turn 0.9856°/day with the Sun and fixes the imaging time all year.</mark>

</Note>

## 2.6 Ground tracks, repeat cycles and coverage

The <Term id="ground-track">ground track</Term> is the path traced by the point directly below the satellite (nadir). The highest latitude it reaches equals i (or 180° − i for retrograde orbits). Because the Earth turns 360° in a sidereal day, each orbit's track shifts west:

<Formula label="Westward track shift per orbit" tex="\Delta L = 360^\circ \times \frac{T}{86\,164\ \text{s}}" where="
T :: orbital period (s)
\Delta L :: longitude shift of the track between consecutive orbits (ignoring node drift)
" />

During one 100.7-minute Sentinel-2 orbit the Earth turns 25.2°, so the next track is 25.2° further west (about 2810 km at the equator). Sentinel-2's swath is 290 km, much narrower than that gap, so a single satellite needs many days for its tracks to fill the gaps.

### Repeat cycle and revisit

An orbit **repeats** when after exactly k orbits in d days the satellite is back on the same track. The altitude is tuned to satisfy this; that is the <Term id="repeat-cycle">repeat cycle</Term>:

- **Landsat 8/9:** 233 orbits / 16 days (period 98.9 min), altitude 705 km. Swath 185 km × 233 = 43,105 km, just enough to cover the 40,075 km equator. The two satellites are 8 days apart, so a point is imaged every 8 days.
- **Sentinel-2:** 143 orbits / 10 days, 786 km, 290 km swath. Two satellites 180° apart give a 5-day revisit at the equator, shorter at mid-latitudes where swaths overlap.
- **Sentinel-1:** 175 orbits / 12 days, 693 km. An exactly repeating track matters for InSAR (Chapter 16).

Sentinel-2 images only on the **descending pass**, when the ground below is in daylight. It crosses the equator at 10:30 local time and its track repeats exactly after 10 days.

With two satellites each point is imaged **2.23 times** on average in 10 days. That is why ESA says "Sentinel-2 revisits every 5 days".

<Note kind="myth">

"Repeat cycle and revisit are the same thing." The **repeat cycle** is the number of days until the ground track coincides again, a property of the orbit. The **<Term id="revisit">revisit</Term>** is the gap between two *possible* acquisitions of a point. It is much shorter with more satellites, where swaths overlap at high latitudes, or when the satellite tilts to image off-nadir: WorldView-3 can image a point almost daily at large tilt angles, at the cost of coarser resolution and more distorted geometry.

</Note>

Being imaged is not the same as having a usable image. Chapter 9 showed that in Hanoi only 92 of 433 dates in 2022–2024 were clear enough. At Hanoi's latitude the satellite passes at about **10:43** local time, just when summer convective clouds start to build.

### Visibility geometry: elevation, central angle and slant range

A ground station or user dish works only when the satellite is above a **minimum elevation ε** over the horizon: near the horizon buildings, trees and hills block the view, and the signal crosses more atmosphere. For a satellite at radius r = R + h, three quantities describe what is in view: the central angle λ (half the coverage, seen from the Earth's centre), the nadir angle η at the satellite and the <Term id="slant-range">slant range</Term> d:

<Formula label="Central angle, nadir angle and slant range" tex="\lambda = \arccos\!\left(\frac{R\cos\varepsilon}{R+h}\right) - \varepsilon, \qquad \sin\eta = \frac{R\cos\varepsilon}{R+h}, \qquad \lambda + \eta + \varepsilon = 90^\circ" where="
\varepsilon :: minimum elevation of the ground station
\lambda :: Earth central angle from nadir to the edge of coverage; coverage radius along the ground = R·λ (λ in radians)
\eta :: nadir angle: how far off vertical the satellite must look to see the edge
" />

<Formula label="Slant range and visible share of the surface" tex="d = \sqrt{(R+h)^2 - R^2\cos^2\varepsilon} - R\sin\varepsilon, \qquad f = \frac{1-\cos\lambda}{2}" where="
d :: distance from a station at the edge of coverage to the satellite (km)
f :: share of the Earth's surface inside the coverage (spherical cap)
" />

## 2.7 Constellations

A single LEO satellite sees a small area and passes in a few minutes. Continuous coverage or frequent revisits need several satellites working together: a <Term id="walker-constellation">constellation</Term>. The most common layout is the **Walker Delta**, written i : t/p/f: t satellites spread evenly over p planes spaced 360°/p in Ω, with f the phasing between adjacent planes.

- **Galileo:** 56° : 24/3/1 (ESA). **GPS** uses 6 planes inclined at 55°.
- **Starlink**, original 550 km shell: 72 planes × 22 satellites at 53°.
- **Sentinel-2** is the smallest constellation: two satellites in one plane, 180° apart (Figure 2.9).

A satellite at inclination i spends the most time near latitudes ±i, where its track "turns around". So Starlink's 53° shell is densest around 40–53° latitude; Viet Nam (8–23°N) sits in a sparser zone but is still covered. Chapter 6 goes deeper into constellation design: Walker patterns, latency, how many satellites are needed, and a globe with the real Starlink fleet.

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">One satellite is limited by its coverage and repeat cycle; continuous coverage or denser revisits need more satellites and more planes.</mark>

</Note>
