## 10.1 What a coordinate needs

The Earth is not a perfect sphere. To write positions as numbers we replace it with an **ellipsoid**, a sphere flattened at the poles. We then choose how this ellipsoid sits relative to the real Earth; that choice is the <Term id="datum">datum</Term>. Finally, to measure distances in metres on paper or a screen, we flatten the curved surface with a **projection**.

Together these make a <Term id="crs">coordinate reference system (CRS)</Term>. Every GIS package and Google Earth Engine names a CRS by its EPSG code.

<Note kind="key">

Ellipsoid + datum + projection = CRS. A coordinate pair means something only together with its CRS, usually written as an EPSG code.

</Note>

<Depth level="ref" title="Reference: EPSG codes common in Viet Nam">

| EPSG | Name | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| 4326 | WGS 84 (lat/lon) | GPS, Google Earth, GeoJSON |
| 32648 / 32649 | WGS 84 / UTM zone 48N / 49N | Sentinel-2 and Landsat tiles (this handbook uses 32648) |
| 4756 | VN-2000 (lat/lon) | National datum |
| 3405 / 3406 | VN-2000 / UTM zone 48N / 49N | Small-scale topographic maps |
| 5897 | VN-2000 / TM-3 zone 482 (central meridian 105°) | Cadastral maps, e.g. Hanoi |
| 9210 | VN-2000 / TM-3 105-45 | Cadastral maps, e.g. Ho Chi Minh City |

Source: EPSG dataset (IOGP), queried through PROJ.

</Depth>

## 10.2 WGS84, VN-2000 and the datum shift

GPS and most satellite imagery use **WGS84**. Since 2000 Viet Nam has used <Term id="vn2000">VN-2000</Term> (Decision 83/2000/QĐ-TTg), replacing the older HN-72. VN-2000 uses the **same ellipsoid** as WGS84 but shifts it to fit Viet Nam best. The same point therefore has two different pairs of latitude and longitude.

Converting between them takes a <Term id="helmert">7-parameter Helmert transformation</Term>, with the parameters published in Circular 973/2001/TT-TCĐC.

<div class="worked">

#### Example: one point at Hoan Kiem Lake in four coordinate systems

WGS84 point: 105.8342°E, 21.0278°N.

1. **UTM zone 48N (WGS84):** E = 586,683.64 m, N = 2,325,450.79 m. Point scale factor k = 0.9996929.
2. **Shift to the VN-2000 datum** (7-parameter Helmert): 105.8323173°E, 21.0287858°N. Longitude decreases, latitude increases.
3. **VN-2000 / UTM 48N:** E = 586,487.43 m, N = 2,325,558.87 m. Compared with step 1 the point moves **ΔE = −196.2 m, ΔN = +108.1 m, 224.0 m in total**.
4. **VN-2000 / TM-3, central meridian 105°, k₀ = 0.9999:** E = 586,513.38 m, N = 2,326,256.82 m. The northing is about 700 m larger than in step 3 only because of the different k₀ (0.9999 versus 0.9996).

<mark>Lesson: an E, N pair means nothing without the name of its system.</mark> A 224 m error is a city block; 700 m can be a different ward.

</div>

<Depth level="advanced" title="The Helmert formula and the rotation-sign trap">

From VN-2000 to WGS84 in Earth-centred 3-D coordinates (X, Y, Z):

<Formula label="7-parameter Helmert transformation" tex="\begin{aligned} \mathbf{X}_{WGS84} &= \mathbf{T} \\ & + (1 + s)\,\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{X}_{VN2000} \end{aligned}" where="
\mathbf{X} :: Earth-centred 3-D coordinates (X, Y, Z)
\mathbf{T} :: translation vector (m)
s :: scale factor (ppm)
\mathbf{R} :: rotation matrix from the three angles rx, ry, rz
" />

Parameters (EPSG:6960): T = (−191.90441429, −39.30318279, −111.45032835) m; rotations rx = −0.00928836″, ry = 0.01975479″, rz = −0.00427372″; s = 0.252906278 ppm.

<Note kind="warn" label="The rotation-sign trap">

The rotation matrix R comes in two conventions, **coordinate frame** and **position vector**, which differ only in the sign of the rotations. EPSG lists these parameters in the coordinate-frame convention. Entering them in software that expects the other convention without flipping the signs gives errors of a few decimetres.

</Note>

The handbook's own function agrees with PROJ to better than 1e-8 degrees (about 1 mm).

The EPSG dataset also holds a second parameter set, EPSG:5194 "VN-2000 to WGS 84 (1)", and PROJ may pick it depending on location: at Ben Thanh Market the two sets differ by about 0.8 m. When sub-metre accuracy matters, state which transformation code you used.

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

VN-2000 and WGS84 share an ellipsoid but differ in datum. Typing VN-2000 numbers into a WGS84 map moves the Hoan Kiem Lake point by 224 m; converting between them takes the 7-parameter Helmert transformation.

</Note>

## 10.3 Projections and scale error

A curved surface cannot be flattened without distortion. The <Term id="utm">UTM projection</Term> divides the Earth into zones 6° wide. In each zone, lengths are exact along two lines about 180 km either side of the central meridian. Most of Viet Nam lies in **zone 48** (102°–108°E, central meridian 105°); the eastern part from 108°E is in **zone 49**.

The <Term id="scale-factor">scale factor k</Term> says how much the grid enlarges or shrinks. On the UTM central meridian k = 0.9996 (shrinking by 400 ppm, i.e. 40 cm per km). Towards the zone edges k exceeds 1.

| Place | Longitude | UTM zone | k | Error over 1 km |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Da Nang | 108.22°E | 49 | 1.000694 | +69 cm |
| Ca Mau | 104.75°E | 48 | 0.999609 | −39 cm |

</details>

An error of 77 cm per km does not matter when measuring rice area on 10 m imagery. It is far too large for cadastral maps: a 100 m parcel would be 7.7 cm off. Large-scale VN-2000 maps therefore use **3° zones (TM-3) with k₀ = 0.9999**, with a central meridian chosen per province, e.g. 105° for Hanoi. Within a 3° zone the error stays between −100 and about +200 ppm. At Hoan Kiem Lake the TM-3 scale factor is 0.9999925, practically true scale.

<Note kind="key">

Across Viet Nam UTM is off by a few tens of centimetres per kilometre (up to +77 cm at Mong Cai): negligible for 10 m imagery, too much for cadastre. Large-scale maps therefore use VN-2000 TM-3 with k₀ = 0.9999 and a central meridian per province.

</Note>

## 10.4 Raster and vector

The same lake can be stored in two ways:

- <Term id="raster">Raster</Term>: a regular grid of cells, each marked "water" or "not water". Satellite images, DEMs and index maps are rasters.
- <Term id="vector">Vector</Term>: a polygon storing the coordinates of every shoreline vertex. Administrative boundaries, roads and land parcels are usually vectors.

Three lessons:

1. **Area is robust, perimeter is not.** Going from 2,403 to 61 vertices reduces the area by only 0.2 %, but the perimeter loses almost 26 % because the shoreline's wiggles are straightened.
2. The 10 m raster gives 5.128 km², the vector 5.100 km². The vector outline runs midway between shoreline pixel centres, so it trims half a pixel corner at every stair step.
3. A raster spends storage on non-water too (262,144 cells for 51,276 water cells). A vector stores only the shoreline and is much more compact, but it cannot hold continuously varying values such as elevation or NDVI.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why does the raster area wobble as pixels grow?">

At 40 m the area rises to 5.186 km², then falls to 5.018 km² at 320 m. The "≥ 50 % water" rule rounds every shoreline cell up or down. With small cells the rounding errors cancel and the total is stable. With large cells each rounding adds or removes a big patch, so the total depends on where the grid happens to fall. This is the mixed-pixel effect of Chapter 9, seen from the GIS side.

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

Rasters suit continuously varying values (imagery, elevation, NDVI); vectors store boundaries compactly. Areas from raster and vector agree closely, while shoreline length depends strongly on simplification.

</Note>
