## 13.2 Tree-cover loss in the Central Highlands, 2001–2025

The <Term id="tree-cover-loss">Global Forest Change</Term> dataset of Hansen et al. (2013) classifies the whole Landsat archive to find each 30 m pixel that lost tree cover and the year it happened. It is updated every year; this chapter uses version 1.13 (to 2025).

How the handbook computes it:

1. "Forest in 2000" = pixels with canopy cover ≥ 30 % in 2000.
2. "Loss" = forest-2000 pixels flagged as lost in 2001–2025.
3. Areas are summed by year and province, within the five Central Highlands provinces as they were before the 2025 provincial reorganisation (FAO GAUL 2015).

The five provinces had **3.16 million ha** of tree cover in 2000. In 2001–2025, **709 thousand ha** were lost, i.e. **22.5 %**.

| Province | Tree cover 2000 (ha) | Lost 2001–2025 (ha) | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kon Tum | 748,317 | 187,328 | 25.0 % |
| Gia Lai | 720,950 | 175,908 | 24.4 % |
| Dak Lak | 545,958 | 119,858 | 22.0 % |
| Dak Nong | 424,699 | 126,120 | 29.7 % |
| Lam Dong | 716,889 | 99,935 | 13.9 % |

The region's peak year was 2010 (66,251 ha). By period: 281 thousand ha in 2001–2010, 356 thousand ha in 2011–2020 and 72 thousand ha in 2021–2025.

**Reading the numbers correctly:**

- "Tree-cover loss" is **not the same as deforestation**. Hansen records every stand-replacing disturbance: clearing of natural forest, but also rotational harvest of plantations, fire and storm damage. An acacia plantation that is harvested and replanted still counts as "loss".
- Regrowth after 2000 is not subtracted, so 22.5 % is not the net decline of forest area.
- The algorithm has been improved across updates, and the dataset documentation advises caution when comparing years before and after about 2011. Read the difference between periods above as a trend, not a conclusion.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why not a 10 % or 50 % canopy threshold?">

The canopy threshold decides what counts as "forest". A low threshold adds orchards and shrubland; a high one keeps only closed forest. FAO's forest definition uses 10 %; many studies using Hansen choose 25–30 %. Changing the threshold changes both the 2000 area and the loss. Exercise 1 in section 13.6 asks you to recompute with other thresholds.

</Depth>

## 13.3 Ho Chi Minh City urban heat island

Concrete and metal roofs absorb much radiation and evaporate little water, so they get hotter than vegetation and water. This is the <Term id="urban-heat-island">urban heat island</Term>. Landsat's thermal band measures <Term id="lst">land surface temperature (LST)</Term> at 100 m (resampled to 30 m in the Level-2 product).

Mean by land cover in the 40 × 40 km frame:

| Land cover | Mean LST | Share of the frame |
|---|---|---|
| Built-up | 40.9 °C | 37.4 % |
| Bare | 39.7 °C | 1.4 % |
| Grassland | 38.9 °C | 12.5 % |
| Tree cover | 36.8 °C | 21.8 % |
| Cropland | 36.6 °C | 13.0 % |
| Water | 32.3 °C | 10.4 % |
| Mangroves | 32.3 °C | 3.5 % |

Built-up land is **4.1 °C** warmer than tree cover and **8.6 °C** warmer than water. The area with LST ≥ 42 °C is **232.5 km²**, about 15 % of the frame. The frame median is 38.4 °C; 90 % of pixels lie between 30.7 and 44.2 °C.

**Caveats:**

- LST is the temperature **of the surface** at overpass time (around 10 a.m.), not the air temperature measured at weather stations. A metal roof can be tens of degrees hotter than the air.
- The median of 42 dry-season scenes gives a "typical" picture, not the hottest day.
- WorldCover is from 2021 while LST is from 2024, so areas built after 2021 are assigned the wrong class.

## 13.4 Air quality: NO₂ from Sentinel-5P

NO₂ comes mainly from burning fuel (traffic, coal power plants, industry) and biomass. The TROPOMI instrument on Sentinel-5P measures the spectrum of sunlight scattered back by the atmosphere, from which the <Term id="tropospheric-no2">tropospheric NO₂ column</Term> is retrieved: the number of NO₂ molecules from the ground to the top of the troposphere per square metre. Pixels are about 5.5 × 3.5 km.

2024 means within a 10 km radius: **Hanoi 83.8**, **Ho Chi Minh City 61.9** and **the Lai Chau highlands 20.2 µmol/m²**. Central Hanoi is **4.1 times** the rural mountain site.

Three readings from Figure 13.5 (monthly means over 2019–2024):

1. **Season:** Hanoi peaks in December (102.9) and is lowest in July (61.1); HCMC peaks in January (84.5) and is lowest in August (41.6). In summer, strong sunlight destroys NO₂ faster and rain washes it out; in winter the lowest layer of the atmosphere is shallow, so pollutants disperse poorly. This explanation comes from general literature; the handbook does not separate the causes.
2. **April 2020:** Hanoi reads only 58.0, against 86.9 (April 2019) and 90.6 (April 2021). This **coincides** with the nationwide social-distancing period of April 2020. Coincidence in time does not prove causation, because monthly weather also changes NO₂.
3. **Lai Chau** is low all year but peaks in March (33.2), the end of the dry season, when field burning and biomass fires are common in the northern mountains and neighbouring countries. This is an interpretation not checked against fire data.

<Depth level="advanced" title="A NO₂ column is not a ground-level concentration">

µmol/m² is the NO₂ in the **whole air column**, not the µg/m³ concentration a monitoring station measures a few metres above the ground. Converting one into the other needs the vertical NO₂ profile, usually from an atmospheric model. Satellite maps are therefore good for comparing **places and trends**, but do not replace stations when checking air-quality standards.

</Depth>

## 13.5 General lessons

- Start from the **question**, then choose the data: seeing through clouds (radar), a long history (Landsat) or measuring a gas (TROPOMI).
- Every number comes with a **definition**: 30 % canopy, dry-season median LST, tropospheric NO₂ column. Change the definition and the number changes.
- Remote-sensing results need **validation** (field data, stations, statistics) before they inform decisions.
