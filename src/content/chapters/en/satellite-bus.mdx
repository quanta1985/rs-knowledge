## 4.1 Bus and payload

An Earth-observation satellite has two parts. The **<Term id="payload">payload</Term>** is the reason for launching it: a camera, a radar, a GNSS receiver or a communications transponder. The **<Term id="satellite-bus">bus</Term>** is everything else that keeps the payload alive and working:

<Tiles
  items={[
    { t: 'EPS', d: 'Solar arrays, battery, power distribution' },
    { t: 'ADCS', d: 'Star trackers, gyros, reaction wheels' },
    { t: 'Thermal', d: 'MLI blankets, radiators, heaters' },
    { t: 'Propulsion', d: 'Hydrazine or electric thrusters' },
    { t: 'OBC', d: 'Computer, flight software, image memory' },
    { t: 'TT&C', d: 'Commands up, telemetry down, S-band' },
  ]}
  sum="Add the structure: seven bus subsystems, plus the payload makes eight"
  note="Each subsystem has its own budget: mass, power, heat, data, Δv"
/>

Many manufacturers sell ready-made buses and fit the customer's payload. VNREDSat-1, Viet Nam's first optical Earth-observation satellite (2013), was built by Astrium (now Airbus) on the AstroSat-100 bus and carries the NAOMI camera with 2.5 m panchromatic and 10 m multispectral imagery.

<Note kind="key">

A good image is not only the camera's doing. Without enough power there is no image, a pointing error shifts it, a temperature drift moves the focus, and without a ground station the image never leaves memory. <mark class="key">A payload is only as good as the bus that carries it.</mark>

</Note>

The smaller the satellite, the cheaper and faster it is to build, but it has less power, less room for antennas and optics, poorer pointing and often no propulsion. The next sections take these limits one by one.

## 4.2 Power: solar arrays, batteries and Earth's shadow

In space, each square metre facing the Sun receives **1361 W** (the solar constant). Triple-junction GaAs cells convert about 28 % of it into electricity, silicon cells about 15 %. But on every orbit the satellite passes through **Earth's shadow** and must live on its battery.

  why="35.7 %, i.e. 35.3 minutes of a 98.8-minute period. In low orbit Earth blocks a large part of the sky seen from the satellite. Higher up the dark share shrinks slowly: 34.9 % at 786 km." />

<Note kind="myth">

"Every orbit is half day, half night." No: at 700 km the dark share is at most 35.7 %, because from altitude the satellite still sees the Sun after the ground below has entered night. And at large beta angles some orbits are almost never in shadow.

</Note>

The shadow share depends on the **<Term id="beta-angle">beta angle</Term>**, the angle between the Sun direction and the orbit plane. At beta 0 the satellite crosses the middle of the shadow. The larger beta, the closer it skims the shadow's edge, and above a critical value there is no eclipse at all (about 64° at 700 km).

<Formula label="Share of the orbit in shadow (cylindrical shadow)" tex="f_e = \frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{h^2 + 2R_\oplus h}}{(R_\oplus + h)\cos\beta}\right), \qquad f_e = 0 \ \text{if}\ |\sin\beta| \ge \frac{R_\oplus}{R_\oplus + h}" where="
f_e :: share of the period in Earth's shadow
h :: altitude of the circular orbit
R_\oplus :: Earth radius, 6378 km
\beta :: beta angle
" />

A sun-synchronous orbit keeps beta nearly constant through the year. Sentinel-2 crosses the equator at 10:30 a.m. (descending node), with beta about 22° at the equinox, so it still spends 33.8 minutes in shadow per orbit. Sentinel-1 flies a **dawn–dusk** orbit (ascending node at 18:00): the orbit plane is nearly perpendicular to sunlight, beta ranges from about 58° to 90° with the seasons, and eclipse never exceeds 19.2 % of an orbit. A radar makes its own illumination, so it picks the orbit with the most power.

The array must do two jobs: feed the loads in sunlight and recharge what the battery gave up in shadow. Energy routed through the battery loses more, so the eclipse share is divided by a lower efficiency:

<Formula label="Required array power (SMAD)" tex="P_{sa} = \frac{P_e T_e / X_e + P_d T_d / X_d}{T_d}" where="
P_e,\ P_d :: load in eclipse and in sunlight (W)
T_e,\ T_d :: time in eclipse and in sunlight per orbit
X_e,\ X_d :: path efficiency through the battery (0.6) and direct (0.8)
" />

<div class="worked">

#### Example: an array for a 300 W load at 786 km

1. Period 100.6 min; with beta 22.2° the satellite is in shadow for T<sub>e</sub> = 33.8 min and in sunlight for T<sub>d</sub> = 66.8 min.
2. P<sub>sa</sub> = (300 × 33.8 / 0.6 + 300 × 66.8 / 0.8) / 66.8 ≈ **628 W**.
3. One square metre of 28 % GaAs gives 1361 × 0.28 × 0.77 ≈ 293 W, so about **2.14 m²** at beginning of life. Cells also degrade under radiation, so real designs add margin for end of life.

</div>

The battery has limits too. **<Term id="depth-of-discharge">Depth of discharge</Term>** (DoD) is the share of capacity used on each pass through the shadow. At 700 km a satellite cycles its battery 14.6 times a day, 5325 times a year; deeper discharges wear the battery out faster, so LEO designs usually keep DoD low, a few tens of percent.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Beta angle of a sun-synchronous orbit">

Beta follows from the inclination i, the right-ascension difference Ω − α☉ between the ascending node and the Sun, and the Sun's declination δ☉:

<Formula tex="\sin\beta = \cos\delta_\odot \sin i \,\sin(\Omega - \alpha_\odot) + \sin\delta_\odot \cos i" />

For a sun-synchronous orbit Ω − α☉ is fixed by the node's local time: a 10:30 descending node means a 22:30 ascending node, so Ω − α☉ = 157.5°.

</Depth>

## 4.3 Attitude control: looking at the right place

A satellite has nothing to push against, so to turn the camera it must turn the whole spacecraft. The **<Term id="adcs">attitude determination and control system</Term>** (ADCS) does two jobs: **determine** where the satellite points, and **control** it to turn where it must look.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Sensor | Measures | Typical accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| Sun sensor | Sun direction | 0.005° to 3° |
| Horizon sensor (infrared) | direction to Earth's centre | 0.1° to 1° in LEO |
| Magnetometer | Earth's magnetic field, against a model | 1° to 5° |
| <Term id="star-tracker">Star tracker</Term> | images star fields, matches a catalogue | 1 arcsecond to 1 arcminute |
| Gyroscope | rotation rate, between star fixes | drift 0.003 °/h to 1 °/h |
| GNSS receiver | position, velocity, time | a few metres |

</div>

The actuators are: **<Term id="reaction-wheel">reaction wheels</Term>**, which speed up or slow down so the body turns the other way (conservation of angular momentum), usually four so that three axes remain if one fails; **<Term id="bus-cmg">control moment gyroscopes</Term>** (CMG), which keep a wheel spinning and tilt its axis to produce large torques, used on agile satellites such as Pléiades and on the International Space Station; **<Term id="magnetorquer">magnetorquers</Term>**, which push against Earth's field to dump the momentum that disturbances (drag, radiation pressure, gravity gradient) store in the wheels; and small thrusters for large torques or when the wheels saturate.

When something goes wrong, the software puts the satellite into **<Term id="bus-safe-mode">safe mode</Term>**: payload off, arrays pointed at the Sun, battery kept warm, waiting for commands from the ground.

How small must the pointing error be? Compare it with the pixel. At nadir a small angle Δθ (radians) moves the ground point by about h · Δθ:

<Formula label="Pointing error in metres on the ground (nadir)" tex="\Delta x \approx h\,\Delta\theta, \qquad 1'' = 4.85\ \mu\text{rad}" where="
\Delta x :: offset on the ground
h :: satellite altitude
\Delta\theta :: angular error in radians
" />

For VNREDSat-1 at about 678 km, 1 arcsecond is 3.29 m on the ground, so one 2.5 m panchromatic pixel is only 0.76 arcseconds. No satellite holds its pointing that well by hardware alone; images are corrected on the ground with control points and a terrain model (Chapter 17). Sentinel-2's geolocation requirement is under 20 m (2σ) without ground control, equivalent to 5.2 arcseconds from 786 km; ESA's quality reports show about 12 m in practice.

<Note kind="key">

Star trackers know the direction to a few arcseconds, reaction wheels turn the satellite, magnetorquers dump excess momentum. The residual error, a few to a few tens of metres on the ground, is fixed with control points during processing.

</Note>

<Note kind="why" label="Why do small satellites struggle to take sharp images?">

Reaction wheels, good star trackers and large optics are heavy and power-hungry. A 50 kg satellite such as MicroDragon cannot carry the ADCS of a one-tonne spacecraft, so it is hard both to point accurately and to stay steady (free of "jitter") during an exposure.

</Note>

## 4.4 Thermal control

There is no convection in a vacuum. Heat moves only by conduction inside the satellite and by **radiation** to the outside. Each surface takes in sunlight according to its **solar absorptance** α and sheds heat according to its infrared **<Term id="emissivity">emittance</Term>** ε. When heat in equals heat out, the surface reaches its equilibrium temperature:

<Formula label="Radiative balance of a Sun-facing flat plate (Stefan–Boltzmann law)" tex="\alpha S + q = \varepsilon \sigma T^4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad T = \left(\frac{\alpha S + q}{\varepsilon\sigma}\right)^{1/4}" where="
\alpha :: solar absorptance
\varepsilon :: infrared emittance
S :: solar constant, 1361 W/m²
q :: heat from equipment inside (W/m²)
\sigma :: Stefan–Boltzmann constant, 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴)
" />

With no internal heat, the temperature depends only on **the ratio α/ε**. An ideal black plate (α = ε = 1) reaches 120 °C. White paint (α/ε = 0.22) stays at −3 °C, an OSR mirror at −52 °C. Conversely, polished aluminium (α/ε = 3) reaches 245 °C and gold 349 °C: shiny metal absorbs little sunlight but sheds heat even worse.

On every orbit a surface heats up in sunlight and cools in shadow. For a 2 mm white-painted aluminium plate at 700 km, beta 0°, the temperature swings from −70 °C to −7 °C; with 100 W/m² of heat from equipment inside, the range becomes −37 °C to 18 °C. Thermal engineers use:

- **<Term id="bus-mli">Multi-layer insulation</Term>** (MLI): tens of thin metallised films separated by netting, which almost stop radiation. The familiar "gold foil" is usually amber Kapton film aluminised on the back.
- **<Term id="bus-radiator">Radiators</Term>** coated with OSR mirrors or white paint (low α, high ε), placed on faces that see little sunlight.
- **Heat pipes** that carry heat from hot units to the radiators, and thermostatic **heaters** that keep batteries and optics warm in shadow.
- **Active cooling** with cryocoolers for thermal-infrared detectors (Chapter 8).

<Note kind="myth">

"Space is cold, so satellites are always cold." Not really: a vacuum is an excellent insulator, so the usual problem is **getting rid of** equipment heat. A polished aluminium plate in sunlight can exceed 200 °C, while the same plate in shadow slowly cools towards hundreds of degrees below zero.

</Note>

<Note kind="key">

<mark>Surface temperature is set by α/ε, not by colour.</mark> Thermal design means choosing coatings, insulation and radiators so that every unit stays within its allowed range in full sunlight and in eclipse.

</Note>

## 4.5 Spacecraft propulsion

A satellite needs propulsion to raise its orbit after separation, for **<Term id="station-keeping">station keeping</Term>** inside a narrow corridor (so the ground track repeats exactly, Chapter 2), to make up drag, to dodge debris and to deorbit at the end. There are two main families.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Type | Principle | Typical <Term id="specific-impulse">Isp</Term> | Thrust | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold gas | expels compressed gas (nitrogen, butane) | 50–75 s | mN | CubeSats, attitude trim |
| <Term id="bus-hydrazine">Hydrazine monopropellant</Term> | catalytic decomposition | 150–225 s | 0.1 to tens of N | most LEO observation satellites |
| Bipropellant (MMH/NTO) | burns fuel with an oxidiser | 300–340 s | a few N to hundreds of N | GEO satellites, orbit raising |
| <Term id="bus-electric-propulsion">Hall thruster</Term> | ionises an inert gas, accelerates it electrically | about 1500–2000 s | tens to hundreds of mN | Starlink, all-electric GEO |
| Gridded ion thruster | accelerates ions through electrode grids | 2000–5000 s | tens of mN | deep-space probes, GEO |

</div>

The higher the Isp, the less propellant for the same Δv, exactly as the <Term id="rocket-equation">rocket equation</Term> of Chapter 3 says, rewritten for propellant mass:

<Formula label="Propellant for a given Δv" tex="m_p = m_{dry}\left(e^{\Delta v / (I_{sp} g_0)} - 1\right)" where="
m_p :: propellant mass
m_{dry} :: satellite mass with empty tanks
\Delta v :: total velocity change needed (m/s)
I_{sp} :: specific impulse (s)
g_0 :: 9.80665 m/s²
" />

Electric thrusters buy their high Isp with tiny thrust, because thrust is limited by the electric power the arrays can supply:

<Formula label="Thrust of an electric thruster" tex="F = \frac{2\eta P}{g_0 I_{sp}}" where="
F :: thrust (N)
\eta :: overall thruster efficiency
P :: electric power to the thruster (W)
" />

<div class="worked">

#### Example: the argon Hall thruster of Starlink V2 Mini

SpaceX quotes 170 mN at an Isp of 2500 s and 4.2 kW. With η = 0.5: F = 2 × 0.5 × 4200 / (9.80665 × 2500) = **0.171 N**, matching the published value. That is roughly the weight of a small egg; in return the thruster runs for hours a day on very little gas.

</div>

The Δv for **drag make-up** equals the velocity the atmosphere removes each year, and it soars at lower altitude or when the Sun is active. For a ballistic coefficient of 0.01 m²/kg, at 500 km it is 1.1 m/s a year with a quiet Sun but 19 m/s with an active Sun; at 786 km only 0.03–0.6 m/s. A **controlled re-entry** (one burn that drops the perigee to 50 km so the satellite falls into a chosen ocean area) costs about 205 m/s from 786 km, far more than the drag make-up.

<Formula label="Yearly drag make-up Δv (circular orbit)" tex="\Delta v_{yr} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 B \cdot t_{yr}, \qquad B = \frac{C_d A}{m}" where="
\rho :: air density at the orbit altitude
v :: orbital velocity
B :: ballistic coefficient (m²/kg)
t_{yr} :: seconds in a year
" />

<div class="worked">

#### Example: a one-tonne satellite, 7 years at 786 km

1. Drag make-up with an active Sun for 7 years (B = 0.01 m²/kg): 7 × 0.59 ≈ 4 m/s.
2. Controlled re-entry to a 50 km perigee: 205 m/s. Total **209 m/s**.
3. With a dry mass of 1000 kg: hydrazine (220 s) needs **102 kg**, bipropellant (310 s) 71 kg, Hall (1600 s) 13 kg, cold gas (70 s) as much as 356 kg.

Sentinel-2, at the same altitude, carries 123 kg of propellant out of 1140 kg: the same order of magnitude as this rough estimate.

</div>

<Note kind="key">

Chemical thrusters give large thrust and fast response; electric thrusters save propellant many times over but push very slowly. In low LEO drag eats the propellant; in high LEO most of the Δv is reserved for the satellite's own deorbit.

</Note>

## 4.6 Computer, flight software and the radiation environment

The on-board computer (OBC, or the C&DH subsystem) runs the **flight software**: it executes the uploaded command schedule, collects telemetry, drives the ADCS, manages image memory, and compresses and formats data. Most important is **<Term id="bus-fdir">FDIR</Term>** (fault detection, isolation and recovery): the satellite is out of station contact most of the time, so it must handle faults itself, falling back to safe mode if needed.

Electronics in space are bombarded by energetic particles: protons and electrons trapped in the Van Allen belts, particles from solar storms and cosmic rays. They cause three kinds of damage:

<Tiles
  items={[
    { t: 'SEU', d: 'A particle flips one bit in memory or a register. Soft, recoverable' },
    { t: 'Latch-up', d: 'A particle triggers a parasitic path and current surges; power must be cut at once or the chip burns' },
    { t: 'TID', d: 'Accumulated dose drifts component characteristics until they fail over the years' },
  ]}
  sum="The first two are single events; the third is ageing"
/>

Of these, the **<Term id="bus-seu">SEU</Term>** and **<Term id="bus-latch-up">latch-up</Term>** happen one event at a time, while the **<Term id="bus-tid">TID</Term>** builds up over the mission.

In LEO most of these events are concentrated in the **<Term id="bus-saa">South Atlantic Anomaly</Term>** (SAA). Earth's magnetic axis is tilted and offset from the centre, so the field is weakest over South America and the South Atlantic; the inner radiation belt therefore dips lowest there, down to LEO altitudes.

According to IGRF-14, at 500 km the field is weakest, about 18,300 nT, near 22° S, 60° W; over Hanoi it is about 35,300 nT, 1.93 times stronger. At the surface the SAA minimum is about 22,100 nT. Many sensitive instruments (optical detectors, particle counters) pause or flag their data while crossing it.

Mitigations:

- **Radiation-hardened parts**, for example the RAD750 processor family: generations slower than commercial chips but very robust.
- **<Term id="bus-edac">EDAC</Term>**: error-correcting memory, "scrubbed" regularly so flipped bits are fixed before errors pile up.
- **TMR** (triple modular redundancy): three circuits compute the same thing and the majority wins.
- **Watchdogs**: timers that reboot the computer if the software hangs; current limiters that cut power on latch-up.
- **Hot/cold redundancy**: spare computers, wheels and transmitters to switch to after a failure.

NewSpace smallsats often use commercial (COTS) parts, cheaper and faster, compensating with the measures above and accepting more risk. More powerful computers also allow **on-board processing**: discarding cloudy images, compressing, detecting ships in orbit and sending down only what is useful.

<Note kind="key">

Radiation in LEO is not uniform: most bit flips happen while crossing the SAA. Fault-tolerant design (EDAC, TMR, watchdogs, FDIR) matters as much as radiation-hardened parts.

</Note>

## 4.7 Ground segment and data flow

A satellite talks to the ground over two links (details in Chapter 5). The **<Term id="ttc">TT&C</Term>** link (telemetry, tracking and command) is slow but very reliable, usually in S-band (2025–2110 MHz up, 2200–2290 MHz down), following <Term id="ccsds">CCSDS</Term> standards; ranging and Doppler on this link help determine the orbit. Images travel on a separate high-rate link in X-band (8025–8400 MHz, allocated to Earth observation), Ka-band or laser.

A LEO satellite can only communicate while in view of a <Term id="ground-station">ground station</Term>. A sun-synchronous orbit passes close to both poles on every revolution, so a high-latitude station sees almost every orbit, while a station near the equator sees only a few passes a day.

The last step is **<Term id="bus-passivation">passivation</Term>**: venting all stored energy (propellant, pressurant, batteries) so a dead satellite cannot explode into fragments. A retired GEO satellite is raised to a **<Term id="bus-graveyard-orbit">graveyard orbit</Term>** a few hundred kilometres above the geostationary belt; the IADC guidelines set a minimum of 235 km plus a term for solar radiation pressure.

<Note kind="key">

LEOP and commissioning carry the highest risk; routine operations fill almost the whole life of the satellite, with FDIR and safe mode as the safety net. End of life is not "switch off": the satellite must leave useful orbits and be passivated.

</Note>

## 4.9 Drag, collision avoidance and space debris

Low orbits still contain a trace of air. It slows the satellite and lowers the orbit (**<Term id="orbital-decay">orbital decay</Term>**) until, at about 150 km, the satellite re-enters within days. The air density there varies strongly with **solar activity**: an active Sun heats and inflates the thermosphere with ultraviolet light. According to NRLMSIS 2.1, at 400 km the density for an active Sun (F10.7 = 200) is 9.3 times that of a quiet Sun (F10.7 = 70); at 600 km it is 24.5 times.

### Space debris and the Kessler syndrome

Low orbit is getting crowded. CelesTrak's public catalogue (5 Nov 2026) lists 34,344 objects in Earth orbit with orbital elements; 28,936 of them are between 200 and 2000 km. The catalogue only holds objects large enough for radar to track (about 10 cm and up in LEO); smaller fragments are far more numerous.

The 700–900 km band is thick with debris because of two events: China's anti-satellite test against the Fengyun-1C weather satellite on 11 Jan 2007, which created more than 3000 tracked fragments, and the collision between Iridium 33 and the defunct Kosmos 2251 on 10 Feb 2009. On 15 Nov 2021 Russia's anti-satellite test against Kosmos 1408 created more than 1500 fragments. At those altitudes fragments survive for decades to centuries (Figure 4.8).

In 1978 Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais warned of a dangerous threshold, now called the **<Term id="kessler-syndrome">Kessler syndrome</Term>**: once objects are dense enough, each collision creates fragments that cause more collisions, in a chain reaction, until an orbital band becomes unusable.

<Note kind="myth">

"Space is vast, two objects cannot hit each other." In one altitude band thousands of objects cross each other's orbits at relative speeds above 10 km/s; a 1 cm fragment carries the energy of a small explosion. The 2009 Iridium–Kosmos event was a real collision between two intact satellites.

</Note>

### Collision avoidance

Space surveillance centres (such as the US Space Force, publishing through Space-Track) screen every pair of objects and issue **<Term id="conjunction">conjunction</Term>** warnings (Conjunction Data Messages) when two objects are predicted to pass close. The operator then computes the **<Term id="bus-collision-probability">probability of collision</Term>** P<sub>c</sub> from the miss distance, the position uncertainty of both objects and their size:

ESA reports that it has to perform more than one collision-avoidance manoeuvre per satellite per year on average, mostly to avoid debris. Large constellations such as Starlink avoid collisions autonomously (Chapter 6). A satellite without propulsion, like most CubeSats, cannot dodge anything.

### End-of-life rules

The IADC space debris mitigation guidelines have long recommended that LEO satellites leave orbit within **25 years** of the end of their mission. On 29 Sep 2022 the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC, order 22-74) tightened this to **5 years** for satellites licensed by the FCC or granted US market access that end their missions in or pass through the region below 2000 km, after a two-year transition (to 29 Sep 2024). ESA pursues a "Zero Debris" approach by 2030.

On Figure 4.8, with a ballistic coefficient of 0.02 m²/kg, the natural lifetime exceeds 5 years above a starting altitude of about 460 km (quiet Sun) or 620 km (active Sun). Higher than that, the satellite needs propulsion to deorbit itself (Figure 4.5).

<Note kind="key">

<mark class="key">Orbital lifetime grows exponentially with altitude.</mark> The same CubeSat comes down within months to a little over a year from 400 km, but lasts decades to centuries at 700 km. That is why debris at 700–900 km is the most persistent worry, and why collision avoidance and the 5-year rule are now a core part of operations.

</Note>

## 4.10 CubeSats: satellites built to a standard

The **<Term id="cubesat">CubeSat</Term>** was born at California Polytechnic State University (Cal Poly) and Stanford in 1999 from a simple idea: standardise the outer shell so that every small satellite can use the same deployer. One **1U** unit is 10 × 10 cm and 11.35 cm tall. The CubeSat Design Specification (CDS) rev. 14.1 of 2022 allows a 1U to weigh up to 2.00 kg; older revisions capped it at 1.33 kg. Units combine into 2U, 3U, 6U and 12U to make room for larger cameras and arrays.

PicoDragon was a 1U CubeSat built by the Vietnam National Space Center, about 1 kg, released from the ISS Kibo module in 2013. The price of cheap and fast is little power, limited pointing and usually no propulsion: released low, it lives briefly (like PicoDragon); released high, it can become debris for decades.

<Note kind="key">

CubeSats have made space many times cheaper through standardisation, but every budget in this chapter (power, pointing, heat, radiation, communications, end of life) still applies, only tighter.

</Note>
